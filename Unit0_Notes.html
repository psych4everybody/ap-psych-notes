    What you need to be able to do

    Why do you put off your homework until the last minute? Ask seven psychologists and you might get seven different answers: your brain chemistry, your habits, your thoughts, your culture, your childhood, your need for growth, even your ancestors. None of them is "the" answer. Each perspective is a lens, and good psychologists switch between them.

    By the end of this page, you should be able to:

    • Define psychology as a science.
    • Describe the early schools of psychology (background).
    • Explain the seven perspectives: biological, behavioral, cognitive, evolutionary, humanistic, psychodynamic and sociocultural.
    • Compare how different perspectives explain the same behavior, and explain the biopsychosocial model.
    How this shows up on the exam

    You won't be asked to recite the history, but you will be asked to apply and compare the seven perspectives: "Which perspective best explains this?" or "How would a cognitive psychologist explain this differently from a behavioral one?" Every unit after this one uses them.

    Part 1

    What psychology is

    Psychology is the scientific study of behavior (what we do, which can be observed) and mental processes (thoughts, feelings and memories, which have to be inferred). It is built on empiricism: the idea that knowledge should come from careful observation and evidence, not from authority or gut feeling.

    Background: where it started

    Psychology became a science in 1879, when Wilhelm Wundt opened the first psychology laboratory in Germany. Early schools included structuralism (Wundt and Titchener, breaking conscious experience into basic elements using introspection, or looking inward and reporting) and functionalism (William James, asking how mental processes help us adapt). Introspection proved too subjective to replicate, which pushed psychology toward more objective methods.

    Putting the lenses together

    Most behavior is too complex for one lens. The biopsychosocial model combines biological, psychological (thoughts, emotions, learning) and sociocultural factors. Most psychologists take an eclectic approach, drawing on several perspectives at once (you'll meet this again in Unit 5).

    What you need to be able to do

    "I knew it all along." "It's obvious." "Everyone knows that." Our intuition is often wrong, and our brains are very good at convincing us that it isn't. The scientific method is psychology's way of checking ideas against evidence instead of against our hunches.

    By the end of this page, you should be able to:

    • Describe the steps of the scientific method, and tell a theory from a hypothesis.
    • Explain falsifiability and operational definitions.
    • Explain how peer review and replication help science correct itself.
    • Explain confirmation bias, hindsight bias and overconfidence.
    • Explain how cultural norms affect behavior, and the risks of applying psychology in inappropriate or discriminatory ways.
    How this shows up on the exam

    On free-response questions you may be asked to state a study's hypothesis and say whether it is falsifiable, or to identify an operational definition and explain how it allows replication. Hindsight bias and overconfidence are classic multiple-choice scenarios.

    Part 1

    The scientific method

    The scientific method
    Ask a question→Form a hypothesis→Test it (collect data)→Analyze the results→Draw conclusions→Report, review and replicate

    Theory

    A well-tested explanation that organizes many observations and predicts behavior. In science, a theory is not a guess.

    A theory that sleep helps us process and store memories.

    Hypothesis

    A specific, testable prediction, often drawn from a theory.

    "Students who sleep 8 hours after studying will recall more words than students who sleep 4 hours."

    Falsifiable

    A hypothesis is falsifiable if evidence could show it to be wrong. A claim no evidence could ever disprove isn't scientific.

    Falsifiable: "Exercise reduces anxiety scores." Not falsifiable: "Everything happens for a reason."

    Operational definition

    A precise statement of how a variable will be measured in a study, so others can replicate it.

    "Anxiety" = a score on a 20-question anxiety questionnaire; "sleep" = hours recorded by a sleep tracker.

    How science checks itself

    Peer review

    Before publication, other experts examine a study's methods and conclusions for flaws.

    A journal sends a study to three psychologists who question how the sample was chosen.

    Replication

    Repeating a study, often with different participants or settings, to see if the results hold up. Findings that replicate earn more confidence.

    A famous finding fails to replicate in ten new labs, so psychologists rethink it.

    Part 2

    Thinking traps and culture

    Science matters because our everyday thinking has built-in biases:

    Tap a blurred cell to reveal it.
    BiasWhat it isExample
    Confirmation biasLooking for, noticing and remembering information that supports what we already believe, while ignoring evidence against it.Believing your favorite team's referee is unfair, and remembering only the calls that went against you.
    Hindsight biasBelieving, after learning an outcome, that we "knew it all along".After the election result, saying, "It was obvious who would win."
    OverconfidenceBeing more confident in our judgments and knowledge than the evidence justifies.Being sure you'll finish a project in one evening, and needing three.

    Culture and the misuse of psychology

    Cultural norms, expectations and circumstances shape behavior. Making eye contact with a teacher may show respect in one culture and disrespect in another. A finding from one cultural group may not apply to another, so psychologists ask whose behavior a study actually describes.

    Psychological ideas can also be applied in inappropriate or discriminatory ways: using test scores to label whole groups as less able, or calling a classmate "so OCD" for liking things neat, which trivializes a real disorder. Part of thinking like a psychologist is noticing when an idea is being stretched beyond its evidence.

    What you need to be able to do

    Every method in psychology is a trade-off. Some describe behavior in rich detail; some reveal relationships across thousands of people; only one can show cause and effect. The skill here is knowing what each method can, and can't, tell you.

    By the end of this page, you should be able to:

    • Tell experimental and non-experimental methods apart.
    • Describe case studies, correlational studies, naturalistic observation, surveys and meta-analyses, with a strength and a limitation of each.
    • Explain why correlation does not equal causation: the directionality and third variable problems.
    • Tell qualitative and quantitative measures apart, and explain self-report and social desirability bias in surveys.
    How this shows up on the exam

    A very common question: "Which research method is this?" followed by "What can the researchers conclude?" If nothing is manipulated and no one is randomly assigned, it is not an experiment, and you can't conclude cause and effect.

    Part 1

    Experimental and non-experimental methods

    An experiment manipulates an independent variable and uses random assignment to groups (Topic 0.4). Everything else is non-experimental: it can describe and find relationships, but it can't prove cause.

    Tap a blurred cell to reveal it.
    MethodWhat it isStrengthLimitation
    Case studyAn in-depth study of one person or a small group.Rich detail; useful for rare cases.Results may not generalize to others.
    Correlational studyMeasures two or more variables to see whether, and how strongly, they are related.Shows relationships and allows prediction.Can't show cause and effect.
    Naturalistic observationWatching and recording behavior in its natural setting without interfering.Shows real-life behavior.Can't explain why; people may act differently if they know they're watched.

    Being watched changes things

    When people behave differently because they know they're being observed, it's sometimes called the Hawthorne effect. It's one reason observers try to stay unnoticed.

    Part 2

    Correlation is not causation

    Studies often find that students who use their phones more at night get lower grades. Does phone use cause lower grades? A correlation alone can't tell us, for two reasons:

    Directionality problem

    We don't know which variable affects which. Maybe phone use lowers grades, or maybe students who are struggling turn to their phones to escape.

    A ↔ B: which way does it go?

    Third variable problem

    A different, unmeasured variable may cause both. Stress could lead to more phone use and lower grades.

    C causes both A and B.

    Correlation does not equal causation

    If a free-response question describes a correlational study and the conclusion says "causes", "leads to" or "makes", that's your cue to explain the directionality or third variable problem.

    An illusory correlation is believing two things are related when they aren't, often because a few memorable cases stand out: "Every time I wear these socks, we win."

    Part 3

    Measures and surveys

    Qualitative measures

    Collect non-numerical data: words, descriptions, themes. A structured interview asks every participant the same questions in the same order.

    Interviewing students about how they feel on exam days, then looking for common themes.

    Quantitative measures

    Collect numerical data that can be analyzed statistically. A Likert scale asks people to rate their agreement on a scale.

    "I feel stressed before exams": 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree).

    Survey pitfalls

    Self-report bias

    People may not report their own behavior accurately because of poor memory or poor self-insight.

    Students underestimate how many hours a day they spend on their phones.

    Social desirability bias

    People answer in ways that make them look good, rather than truthfully.

    Overreporting how often you recycle or read for pleasure.

    Wording effects

    The way a question is worded can change the answers.

    "Should schools ban phones?" gets different answers from "Should students be allowed to keep phones for emergencies?"

    What you need to be able to do

    Does caffeine really help you remember more? To answer a cause-and-effect question, you need an experiment, and a good one has to rule out every other explanation. This topic is the toolkit for doing exactly that.

    By the end of this page, you should be able to:

    • Identify the independent, dependent and confounding variables in a study.
    • Explain experimental and control groups, and the purpose of random assignment.
    • Explain placebos and the placebo effect.
    • Explain single-blind and double-blind procedures, and the biases they control: experimenter bias and social desirability bias.
    How this shows up on the exam

    Almost every AP exam includes a research scenario where you must identify the IV, DV, a confounding variable, the control group, and whether the study was single- or double-blind. Practice saying each one in a full sentence tied to the scenario.

    Part 1

    Variables and groups

    The basic experiment
    Participants→Random assignment→Experimental group gets the IV / control group doesn't→Measure the DV→Compare the groups
    Tap a blurred cell to reveal it.
    TermWhat it isIn a caffeine and memory study

    Dependent variable (DV)The variable that is measured: the presumed effect.The number of words recalled from a list of 30.

    The IV is what I Vary. The DV Depends on the IV. Try: "The effect of [IV] on [DV]."

    Part 2

    Placebos and blind procedures

    Placebo

    An inactive substance or fake treatment that looks like the real one, given to the control group.

    A sugar pill that looks exactly like the caffeine pill.

    Placebo effect

    Changes in behavior or health caused by expecting a treatment to work, not by the treatment itself.

    Placebo-takers recall more words simply because they believe they had caffeine.

    Controlling expectations

    Tap a blurred cell to reveal it.
    ProcedureWho doesn't know which group is which?What it controls
    Single-blindThe participants.Participant expectations, such as the placebo effect and social desirability bias (acting the way they think they should).
    Double-blindBoth the participants and the researchers who interact with them or record the data.Participant expectations and experimenter bias: researchers unintentionally influencing the results.

    A quasi-experiment compares groups that weren't randomly assigned, such as students in two existing classes. It looks like an experiment, but without random assignment it can't firmly show cause and effect.

    Part 3

    Common mix-ups

    Random assignment

    How participants are put into groups. Allows cause and effect.

    Random sampling (0.5)

    How participants are chosen from the population. Allows generalization.
    Assignment = groups; sampling = who's in the study.

    Independent variable

    Manipulated.

    Dependent variable

    Measured.
    If the researcher sets it, it's the IV.

    Single-blind

    Participants don't know.

    Double-blind

    Participants and researchers don't know.
    Double-blind adds protection against experimenter bias.

    Control group

    Gets no treatment or a placebo.

    Confounding variable

    An uncontrolled outside factor.
    The control group is part of the design; a confound is a flaw in it.

    What you need to be able to do

    Imagine you want to know how much sleep teenagers in the UAE get, so you survey the 25 students in your IB Psychology class. Can you say anything about teenagers in the UAE? Sampling is about who takes part in a study, and how far the results can fairly be stretched.

    By the end of this page, you should be able to:

    • Identify the population and the sample in a study.
    • Tell random sampling and convenience sampling apart, and explain a representative sample.
    • Explain sampling bias and how it limits generalizability.
    • Explain why appropriate representation of participants matters for research outcomes.
    How this shows up on the exam

    Expect a scenario asking whether a sample is representative, whether there is sampling bias, and how far the results can be generalized. Don't mix up random sampling (who is chosen for the study) with random assignment (who goes into which group, Topic 0.4).

    Part 1

    Populations and samples

    Population

    The whole group a researcher wants to draw conclusions about.

    All secondary school students in the UAE.

    Sample

    The subset of the population who actually take part.

    The 600 students who complete the survey.

    Tap a blurred cell to reveal it.
    Sampling methodHow participants are chosenEffect on the results
    Random samplingEvery member of the population has an equal chance of being chosen.Most likely to give a representative sample, so results can be generalized.
    Stratified samplingThe population is divided into subgroups (such as grade levels or emirates), and people are randomly sampled from each in proportion.Makes sure key subgroups are represented.
    Convenience samplingChoosing people who are easy to reach.Quick and cheap, but the sample is often unrepresentative.

    A representative sample closely matches the population on important characteristics (age, background, school type). When it doesn't, the study has sampling bias, which limits generalizability: how far the results can be applied to the wider population. Volunteer bias is one source: people who choose to take part may differ from those who don't.

    Part 2

    Why representation matters

    For much of psychology's history, many studies were run on university students from a narrow range of countries. Their findings were then treated as if they described all people. When participants are appropriately represented (different ages, cultures, backgrounds and abilities), results are more accurate and fair, and we find out where findings don't apply.

    A sample size trap

    A big sample isn't automatically a good one. A million people surveyed through one social media app still only represents that app's users. How participants are chosen matters more than how many there are.

    Who is in the study.

    Random assignment

    Which group each participant is in.
    Sampling supports generalization; assignment supports cause and effect.

    Random sampling

    Everyone has an equal chance.

    Convenience sampling

    Whoever is easy to reach.
    Ask: could anyone in the population have been chosen?

    Population

    Everyone you want to know about.

    Sample

    The people you actually study.
    The sample is drawn from the population.

    What you need to be able to do

    Statistics is how psychologists turn a pile of numbers into a conclusion. You don't need to be a math whiz: on the AP exam you calculate a few simple things (mean, median, mode, range) and interpret the rest. The key question is always: what does this number tell us about the people in the study?

    By the end of this page, you should be able to:

    • Calculate the mean, median, mode and range, and interpret the standard deviation and percentile rank.
    • Explain the normal curve, skewed distributions and bimodal distributions.
    • Explain regression toward the mean.
    • Interpret scatterplots and correlation coefficients.
    • Interpret statistical significance and effect size.
    How this shows up on the exam

    Expect to read a table or graph and calculate or interpret. Classic questions: which measure of central tendency best fits skewed data (the median), what a percentile rank means, whether a correlation is strong or weak, and whether a significant result is also a large effect.

    Part 1

    Central tendency and variation

    Descriptive statistics summarize data; inferential statistics help decide whether results are likely to apply beyond the sample (Part 4).

    Which average?

    When there are extreme scores (outliers) or the data are skewed, the median is the best measure of central tendency, because the mean gets pulled toward the outliers. Think of house prices or salaries.

    Part 2

    The normal curve and skew

    Many traits, such as height or intelligence test scores, fall in a normal curve: a symmetrical, bell-shaped distribution where the mean, median and mode are the same and most scores sit near the middle.

    Percentile rank is the percentage of scores at or below a given score. Scoring in the 84th percentile means you scored as well as or better than 84% of test-takers (about 1 SD above the mean on a normal curve).

    Skewed distributions

    Positive skew

    The tail points to the right (toward high scores). Most scores are low; a few very high scores pull the mean above the median.

    Scores on a very hard test: most students score low, a few score very high.

    Negative skew

    The tail points to the left (toward low scores). Most scores are high; a few very low scores pull the mean below the median.

    Scores on a very easy test: most students score high, a few score very low.

    A bimodal distribution has two peaks, which often means two different groups are mixed together: for example, test scores from students who did and didn't attend a review session.

    Follow the tail

    The skew is named after the direction the tail points, not where the hump is. And the mean always gets dragged toward the tail.

    Part 3

    Correlation and regression toward the mean

    A correlation coefficient (r) runs from -1.0 to +1.0. The sign shows the direction; the number shows the strength. -0.85 is just as strong as +0.85.

    Positive correlation

    Both variables move in the same direction.

    More hours of revision, higher exam scores.

    Negative correlation

    The variables move in opposite directions.

    More hours on social media at night, fewer hours of sleep.

    Regression toward the mean

    Regression toward the mean is the tendency for extreme scores to be followed by scores closer to the average as more data are collected. A player who has an amazing game is likely to have a more ordinary one next; a student who does unusually badly on one quiz will probably do better on the next, with or without any intervention.

    Part 4

    Is the result real, and does it matter?

    Statistical significance

    Indicates that a result is unlikely to be due to chance and likely due to the variables studied. Psychologists usually require p < .05: less than a 5% probability that the result happened by chance. It can be calculated for all types of research.

    "The music group scored higher, p = .01": the difference is unlikely to be a fluke.

    Effect size

    Indicates the strength of a relationship (non-experimental) or the size of the difference between conditions (experimental).

    A tutoring program that raises scores a lot has a large effect size.

    0.2 and belowSmall

    0.8 or greaterLarge
    Significant is not the same as big

    With a large enough sample, even a tiny difference can be statistically significant. Always check the effect size to see whether the difference actually matters.

    Common mix-ups

    Positive skew

    Tail right; mean above median.

    Negative skew

    Tail left; mean below median.
    Name it after the tail.

    Mean

    Pulled by outliers.

    Median

    Resists outliers.
    Skewed data? Use the median.

    Statistical significance

    Is it likely real, not chance?

    Effect size

    How big is it?
    One asks "is it real?", the other "does it matter?"

    Strong negative correlation (r = -0.9)

    Strong, opposite directions.

    Weak positive correlation (r = +0.2)

    Weak, same direction.
    The number shows strength; the sign shows direction.

    Percentile rank

    Percentage of scores at or below yours.

    Percentage correct

    How many questions you got right.
    90th percentile doesn't mean 90%.

    The median, because the owner's salary is an outlier that pulls the mean up.
    3. Most students score above 85 on an easy quiz, but a few score below 40. What kind of distribution is this?
    Negatively skewed: the tail points toward the low scores, and the mean is lower than the median.
    4. Which correlation is strongest: +0.45, -0.82 or +0.10?
    -0.82. Strength depends on the size of the number, not the sign.

    The result is unlikely to be due to chance, but the effect is small, so it may not matter much in practice.

    What you need to be able to do

    Some of psychology's most famous studies would never be allowed today. Participants were deceived, frightened or harmed, sometimes with no idea what they had signed up for. Modern research ethics exist so that people (and animals) who help science aren't hurt by it.

    By the end of this page, you should be able to:

    • Explain the role of institutional review for research with humans and non-human animals.
    • Explain informed consent and informed assent.
    • Explain protection from harm, confidentiality and anonymity.
    • Explain when deception and confederates may be used, and the purpose of debriefing.
    How this shows up on the exam

    You'll often be asked to evaluate whether a research scenario followed ethical procedures. Read for the details: Were participants told what to expect? Could they leave? Were minors' parents asked? Were participants debriefed? Was their data kept private?

    Part 1

    Ethical guidelines

    Psychologists follow ethical guidelines from organizations such as the American Psychological Association (APA). Before a study begins, it must be approved by a review board:

    Institutional review board (IRB)

    Reviews research with human participants before it starts, checking that risks are minimized and justified by the benefits.

    A university IRB asks a researcher to remove a stressful task from a study with teenagers.

    Animal care committee (IACUC)

    Reviews research with non-human animals, making sure animals are cared for humanely and suffering is minimized.

    A committee checks the housing and treatment of lab rats in a learning study.

    Informed consentParticipants are told what the study involves, including risks, and agree to take part. They know they can withdraw at any time.Before a stress study, adult participants read and sign a form describing the tasks.
    Informed assentParticipants who can't give legal consent (such as children under 18) agree to take part, and a parent or guardian gives consent.A 15-year-old agrees to a memory study, and her parents sign the consent form.
    Protection from harmResearchers minimize physical and psychological risks, and stop or help if harm occurs.Participants who become upset during a task are offered support and the chance to stop.

    Common mix-ups

    Confidentiality

    Data collected with names, kept private.

    Anonymity

    No names ever collected.
    Can anyone link the data to a person? Confidential: only researchers. Anonymous: no one.

    Informed consent

    Before the study.

    Debriefing

    After the study.
    Consent opens the study; debriefing closes it.

    Informed assent

    The minor agrees.

    Parental consent

    The guardian agrees.
    For minors, you need both.

    IRB

    Reviews human research.
