    What you need to be able to do

    Exam week. Your shoulders are tight, you keep getting headaches, and of course you catch a cold right after the last paper. That is not bad luck. Health psychology studies how what goes on in our minds, our habits and our social lives shows up in our bodies, and stress is its starring example.

    By the end of this page, you should be able to:

    • Explain what health psychology studies.
    • Explain how stress affects health, including eustress and distress, traumatic stress, daily hassles and adverse childhood experiences (ACEs).
    • Explain the general adaptation syndrome (GAS) and the tend-and-befriend response.
    • Tell problem-focused and emotion-focused coping apart.
    How this shows up on the exam

    Expect a scenario asking which phase of the GAS someone is in (the exhaustion phase is where illness is most likely), and whether a coping strategy is problem-focused or emotion-focused. Remember that not all stress is bad: eustress can motivate.

    Part 1

    Stress and health

    Health psychology looks at how psychological, behavioral and social factors affect physical health, illness and wellness: why some people get sick more often, why people find it hard to change unhealthy habits, and how stress gets "under the skin".

    Stress and the body

    Stress makes us more susceptible to disorders and disease. It has been linked to hypertension (high blood pressure), headaches and immune suppression, which is why you are more likely to catch that cold during a stressful week.

    Eustress

    Stress we see as motivating: a challenge that energizes us.

    The nervous excitement before a football final that helps you focus.

    Distress

    Stress we see as debilitating: it overwhelms us and wears us down.

    Feeling paralyzed by a pile of deadlines you can't see a way through.

    Traumatic stress

    Stress from a major, often sudden, threatening event.

    Being in a serious car accident.

    Daily hassles

    Small, everyday irritations that build up over time.

    Traffic every morning, a phone that keeps dying, a noisy neighbor.

    Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) are stressful or traumatic experiences in childhood, such as neglect, losing a parent or living with serious conflict at home. They are sources of stress that can affect a person throughout the lifespan, raising the risk of health and psychological problems in adulthood.

    Part 2

    How we react to stress

    Hans Selye described the general adaptation syndrome (GAS): the process the body goes through when it faces stress.

    Tap a blurred cell to reveal it.
    PhaseWhat happensExample: a two-week exam period
    Alarm reactionThe stressor is first encountered and the body reacts with a fight, flight or freeze response. Resistance briefly dips.The timetable comes out and your heart races.
    ResistanceThe body confronts the stressor, staying highly aroused to cope.You keep going on late nights and adrenaline through the first week.
    ExhaustionThe stress subsides or the body's resources are spent. This is when we are most susceptible to illness.The day after the last exam, you come down with a fever.

    Tend and befriend

    Not everyone fights or flees. The tend-and-befriend theory proposes that some people react to stress by caring for their own needs and the needs of others, and by seeking connection with others. Research suggests this response is seen more often in women.

    Example: after hearing of a family emergency, someone gathers relatives together, makes food for everyone and keeps checking in on each person.

    Part 3

    Coping with stress

    Problem-focused coping

    Seeing the stress as a problem to solve, and working on solutions until one works.

    Stressed about a group project, you make a schedule, divide the tasks and message the teacher with a question.

    Emotion-focused coping

    Managing your emotional reaction to the stress, rather than the stressor itself. Strategies include deep breathing, meditation, or medication aimed at reducing stressful emotions.

    The night before the exam, you do some deep breathing and go for a walk to calm down.

    Which works better?

    It depends on whether the situation can be changed. Problem-focused coping helps when you have control (studying for a test). Emotion-focused coping helps when you don't (waiting for exam results).

    Part 4

    Common mix-ups

    Eustress

    Motivating stress.

    Distress

    Debilitating stress.
    The same event can be either, depending on how the person sees it.

    Resistance phase

    Coping while highly aroused.

    Exhaustion phase

    Resources spent; illness most likely.
    If the person has just got sick after a long stressful stretch, it's exhaustion.

    Problem-focused coping

    Changing the situation.

    Emotion-focused coping

    Changing how you feel about it.
    Ask: is the person tackling the cause, or calming the feeling?

    Traumatic stress

    A major, threatening event.

    Daily hassles

    Small irritations that add up.
    Size and frequency: one big event, or many small ones.

    What you need to be able to do

    For most of its history, psychology focused on what goes wrong: disorders, damage, dysfunction. Positive psychology asks the opposite question. What helps people do well? What makes a life go right? It's a short topic, but it connects to almost everything in this unit.

    By the end of this page, you should be able to:

    • Explain what positive psychology studies.
    • Explain how gratitude and using signature strengths increase subjective well-being.
    • Name the six categories of virtues in the classification of character strengths.
    • Explain posttraumatic growth.
    How this shows up on the exam

    Short questions here usually test the six virtues, the link between gratitude and well-being, or posttraumatic growth. Positive psychology research is often correlational, so watch for questions on what a study can and can't conclude about cause.

    Part 1

    What helps people thrive

    Positive psychology seeks to identify the factors that lead to well-being, resilience, positive emotions and psychological health. Subjective well-being is how satisfied and happy people say they are with their lives.

    Positive subjective experiences

    Gratitude

    Expressing gratitude increases subjective well-being.

    Writing down three things you're thankful for each night, or messaging a teacher to thank them.

    Signature strengths

    People who regularly use their signature strengths (their own best character strengths) report more happiness and higher subjective well-being.

    A naturally kind student who volunteers to help new students settle in.

    Six categories of virtues

    Researchers built a classification of character strengths around six categories of virtues:

    Tap a blurred cell to reveal it.
    VirtueWhat it coversExample strengths
    WisdomStrengths of knowing and thinking.Curiosity, creativity, love of learning.
    CourageStrengths of acting despite fear or difficulty.Bravery, persistence, honesty.
    HumanityStrengths of caring for others.Kindness, love, social intelligence.
    JusticeStrengths of fairness and community.Fairness, teamwork, leadership.
    TemperanceStrengths of self-control and moderation.Forgiveness, humility, self-regulation.
    TranscendenceStrengths that connect us to something larger and give meaning.Gratitude, hope, appreciation of beauty.
    Remember the six

    Wise Courageous Humans Justly Temper Troubles: Wisdom, Courage, Humanity, Justice, Temperance, Transcendence.

    Part 2

    Resilience and posttraumatic growth

    Resilience

    The ability to cope with and recover from difficulty.

    A student who fails a test, figures out what went wrong and bounces back.

    Posttraumatic growth

    Positive psychological change that can come after trauma or serious stress: new appreciation for life, stronger relationships, a clearer sense of purpose.

    After surviving a serious illness, someone says they now value their family more and have found a new direction in life.

    Growth is not the absence of pain

    Posttraumatic growth doesn't mean the trauma was good or that the person no longer struggles. It means that positive change can happen alongside the difficulty.

    Part 3

    Common mix-ups

    Resilience

    Bouncing back to how you were.

    Posttraumatic growth

    Coming out changed for the better.
    Resilience returns to baseline; growth goes beyond it.

    Humanity

    Caring for individuals: kindness, love.

    Justice

    Fairness in groups: teamwork, leadership.
    One-to-one care versus fairness to the community.

    Temperance

    Self-control and moderation.

    Transcendence

    Meaning, hope, gratitude.
    Temperance holds you back from excess; transcendence connects you to something larger.

    What you need to be able to do

    Everyone feels anxious, sad or a bit odd sometimes. So where is the line between a difficult day and a psychological disorder? This topic is about how psychologists draw that line, what labels can do for (and to) people, and how each perspective you met in Unit 0 explains what causes a disorder.

    By the end of this page, you should be able to:

    • Describe how dysfunction, distress and deviation from social norms are used to identify psychological disorders.
    • Explain the positive and negative consequences of diagnosis.
    • Describe the DSM and the ICD, and why diagnosis needs specialized training.
    • Explain how each psychological perspective explains disorders, and why most psychologists take an eclectic approach.
    • Explain the biopsychosocial model and the diathesis-stress model.
    A note before you start

    This topic is about understanding, not diagnosing. Many symptoms on this page are things everyone experiences sometimes; a disorder is about how intense, lasting and disruptive they are, and only a trained professional can decide that. If anything here feels close to home, please talk to the school counselor or an adult you trust.

    How this shows up on the exam

    Expect a scenario asking which perspective a psychologist is taking when explaining a disorder, or asking you to apply the diathesis-stress model. Remember: no single criterion (such as being unusual) is enough on its own to define a disorder.

    Part 1

    Defining and diagnosing disorders

    Psychologists consider several factors together when deciding whether behavior is a psychological disorder:

    Tap a blurred cell to reveal it.
    FactorThe question it asksExample
    DysfunctionDoes it get in the way of everyday life: school, work, relationships, self-care?Anxiety so strong that a student can no longer attend classes.
    DistressDoes the person experience it as upsetting or painful?Constant worry that leaves someone exhausted and unhappy.
    Deviation from social normsIs it very different from what is typical or expected in that culture?Hearing voices that no one else hears.
    Unusual is not the same as disordered

    Deviation alone isn't enough: being a chess prodigy is unusual, but not a disorder. Norms also differ across cultures and change over time, which is why the context matters.

    The double edge of diagnosis

    Positive consequences

    A diagnosis can give access to effective treatment and support, help people understand what they're experiencing, and help professionals communicate and research.

    A student finally diagnosed with ADHD gets extra exam time and learns strategies that help.

    Negative consequences

    Labels can bring stigma and discrimination, change how others treat the person, and shape how people see themselves. Cultural and societal norms, racism, ageism and other biases can also affect who gets diagnosed and how.

    Classmates start treating someone differently once they hear about a diagnosis.

    Classification systems

    Diagnosing a disorder requires specialized training and the use of evidence-based diagnostic tools. Two major classification systems are used, and both are updated regularly as research and practice advance:

    DSM

    The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, developed by the American Psychiatric Association.

    Widely used by clinicians in the United States.

    ICD

    The International Classification of Diseases, developed by the World Health Organization.

    Used around the world, including for health records.

    Part 2

    Explaining the causes of disorders

    Each perspective from Unit 0 points to a different kind of cause. Imagine explaining why someone has a strong fear of driving:

    Tap a blurred cell to reveal it.
    PerspectiveCauses of disorders focus on...Fear of driving
    BehavioralMaladaptive learned associations between stimuli and responses.A minor crash was paired with terror, and the fear became conditioned to cars.
    PsychodynamicUnconscious thoughts and experiences, often from childhood.A buried childhood conflict is being expressed as fear.
    HumanisticA lack of social support and being unable to fulfill one's potential.Feeling unsupported and stuck, the person's fears have taken over.
    CognitiveMaladaptive thoughts, beliefs, attitudes or emotions."If I drive, I will definitely crash."
    EvolutionaryBehaviors and mental processes that reduce the likelihood of survival.A useful alertness to danger has become so extreme that it now gets in the way of normal life.
    SocioculturalMaladaptive social and cultural relationships and dynamics.Family members constantly talk about how dangerous the roads are.
    BiologicalPhysiological or genetic issues.An overactive amygdala, or a family history of anxiety.

    In practice, most psychologists use an eclectic approach: they draw on more than one perspective when diagnosing and treating clients.

    Interaction models

    Biopsychosocial model

    Any psychological problem may involve a combination of biological, psychological and sociocultural factors.

    Depression linked to genes (bio), negative thinking (psych) and social isolation (social).

    Diathesis-stress model

    Disorders develop when a genetic vulnerability (the diathesis) combines with stressful life experiences (the stress).

    Someone with a family history of depression develops it only after losing their job. Their sibling, with the same genes but less stress, doesn't.

    Think of a match

    The diathesis is the match; the stress is the strike. A match on its own doesn't light, and striking nothing doesn't make fire. You need both.

    Part 3

    Common mix-ups

    DSM

    American Psychiatric Association.

    ICD

    World Health Organization.
    D for the Diagnostic manual from America; I for International.

    Behavioral perspective

    Learned associations.

    Cognitive perspective

    Maladaptive thoughts and beliefs.
    Conditioning versus thinking.

    Biopsychosocial model

    Biological + psychological + social factors.

    Diathesis-stress model

    Genetic vulnerability + stress.
    Diathesis-stress is specifically about a predisposition triggered by stress.

    Eclectic approach

    Using several perspectives.

    Biopsychosocial model

    A model of causes.
    Eclectic describes how a psychologist works; biopsychosocial describes what causes a disorder.

    Part 1

    Neurodevelopmental disorders

    Neurodevelopmental disorders begin during the developmental period (childhood). Symptoms are judged by whether a person's behavior is appropriate for their age or maturity.

    Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD)

    A persistent pattern of inattention (difficulty staying focused, losing things, being easily distracted) and/or hyperactivity and impulsivity (fidgeting, interrupting, acting without thinking), beyond what is typical for the person's age.

    A student who can't stay seated, constantly loses homework and blurts out answers, at a level that disrupts school and home life.

    Autism spectrum disorder (ASD)

    Ongoing differences in social communication and interaction, along with restricted, repetitive behaviors or interests. It's a spectrum: support needs range widely from person to person.

    A child who finds back-and-forth conversation hard, follows the same routines every day and knows everything about train timetables.

    Possible causes: environmental, physiological or genetic.

    Part 2

    Schizophrenia spectrum disorders

    Schizophrenia spectrum disorders involve problems in one or more of five areas. Schizophrenia can be acute (sudden, often in response to stress) or chronic (long-lasting).

    Tap a blurred cell to reveal it.
    SymptomPositive or negative?What it looks like
    DelusionsPositiveFalse beliefs, such as delusions of persecution ("Everyone is plotting against me") or grandeur ("I have special powers").
    HallucinationsPositiveFalse perceptions in any sense, most often hearing voices.
    Disorganized thinking or speechPositiveJumping between unrelated ideas, or word salad: words strung together in nonsensical ways.
    Disorganized motor behavior (catatonia)Positive or negativeCatatonic excitement (a positive symptom: agitated, purposeless movement) or catatonic stupor (a negative symptom: little or no movement).
    Negative symptomsNegativeThe absence of typical behavior, such as flat affect (little emotional expression) or lack of movement.
    Positive and negative are not good and bad

    Think math, not mood. Positive symptoms are added to typical experience (voices, false beliefs). Negative symptoms are typical behaviors that have been taken away (emotion, movement, speech).

    Possible causes: a genetic or biological link, such as prenatal exposure to a virus, or imbalances in certain neurotransmitters. The dopamine hypothesis proposes that too much dopamine activity contributes to positive symptoms.

    Part 3

    Depressive and bipolar disorders

    Depressive disorders involve a sad, empty or irritable mood along with physical and cognitive changes (in sleep, appetite, energy, concentration) that affect a person's ability to function.

    Major depressive disorder

    Episodes of at least two weeks of depressed mood or loss of interest in almost everything, with other symptoms such as changes in sleep and energy, feelings of worthlessness and difficulty concentrating.

    Someone who has lost all interest in activities they used to love, sleeps most of the day and feels worthless.

    Persistent depressive disorder

    A milder but long-lasting depressed mood, present most days for at least two years.

    Someone who has felt low and flat most days for years, though still gets through work and school.

    Bipolar disorders involve periods of mania (abnormally elevated mood and energy, little need for sleep, racing thoughts, risky decisions) and periods of depression. Bipolar cycling is moving between these states, and each period can last different amounts of time.

    Bipolar I disorder

    At least one episode of full mania, severe enough to seriously disrupt life (episodes of depression are common too).

    Someone who stays awake for days, spends their savings and believes they will soon be world-famous.

    Bipolar II disorder

    Episodes of hypomania (a milder form of mania) and episodes of major depression.

    Someone who goes through weeks of deep depression, then a few days of unusual energy and productivity.

    Possible causes of depressive and bipolar disorders: biological, genetic, social, cultural, behavioral or cognitive sources.

    Part 4

    Anxiety disorders

    Anxiety disorders involve excessive fear or anxiety with related changes in behavior. Test yourself: hide the disorder names and identify each one.

    Tap a blurred cell to reveal it.
    DisorderKey featureExample
    Specific phobiaIntense fear of a specific object or situation, such as acrophobia (heights) or arachnophobia (spiders).Refusing to visit the observation deck of a tall tower because of fear of heights.
    AgoraphobiaIntense fear of situations where escape might be hard: public transportation, open spaces, enclosed spaces like shops and cinemas, queues or crowds, or being outside the home alone.Avoiding the metro and malls, and eventually not leaving home at all.
    Panic disorderRepeated, unexpected panic attacks: sudden, overwhelming waves of fear with physical symptoms like a racing heart and difficulty breathing.Suddenly feeling as though you're having a heart attack while sitting quietly in class.

    Culture-bound forms

    Ataque de nervios

    A culture-bound form of panic, experienced mainly by people of Caribbean or Iberian descent.

    Sudden episodes of uncontrollable shouting, crying and trembling, often after a family stressor.

    Taijin kyofusho

    A culture-bound form of social anxiety, experienced mainly by Japanese people, involving fear that others judge one's body as undesirable or offensive.

    Fear that one's appearance or body might offend others.

    Possible causes: learned associations between stimuli (conditioning), maladaptive thinking or emotional responses, and biological or genetic sources.

    Part 5

    OCD, dissociative disorders and PTSD

    Obsessive-compulsive and related disorders

    These involve obsessions (unwanted, intrusive thoughts) and compulsions (repetitive behaviors done to address the obsessions).

    Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD)

    Obsessions and compulsions that take up a lot of time and cause distress.

    An intrusive fear of germs (obsession) leads to washing hands until they crack (compulsion).

    Hoarding disorder

    Persistent difficulty throwing things away, whatever their value, leading to cluttered living spaces.

    Rooms so full of old newspapers and bags that they can no longer be used.

    Possible causes: learned associations, maladaptive thinking or emotional responses, and biological or genetic sources.

    Dissociative disorders

    These involve dissociation: a disconnection in consciousness, memory, identity, emotion, perception, body representation, motor control or behavior.

    Dissociative amnesia

    Being unable to recall important personal information, usually linked to trauma or stress. With fugue, the person also travels away or wanders, confused about their identity.

    After a traumatic event, someone can't remember a whole period of their life; with fugue, they turn up in another city unsure how they got there.

    Dissociative identity disorder (DID)

    Two or more distinct identities or personality states, with gaps in memory.

    A person who discovers notes in their own handwriting that they don't remember writing, in a style unlike their own.

    Possible causes: the experience of trauma or stress.

    Trauma and stressor-related disorders

    Posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) follows exposure to a traumatic or stressful event, with lasting psychological distress. Symptoms may include hypervigilance (always on alert), severe anxiety, flashbacks, insomnia, emotional detachment and hostility. Example: someone who survived a building fire jumps at every alarm, has flashbacks of the smoke and can't sleep.

    Possible causes: the experience of trauma or stress.

    Part 6

    Eating and personality disorders

    Feeding and eating disorders

    These involve changes in eating or the absorption of food that harm a person's health or psychological functioning.

    Anorexia nervosa

    Restricting food intake to a dangerous degree, alongside an intense fear of gaining weight and a distorted view of one's own body.

    The key feature is serious restriction and a distorted body image.

    Bulimia nervosa

    Repeated episodes of binge eating followed by behaviors to compensate for it, such as purging.

    The key feature is the cycle of binging and compensating.

    Possible causes: biological, genetic, social, cultural, behavioral or cognitive sources.

    Support

    Eating disorders are serious health conditions, and they are treatable. If you're worried about yourself or a friend, please speak to the school counselor or the school nurse.

    Personality disorders

    Personality disorders are enduring patterns of inner experience and behavior that differ markedly from the person's culture, are pervasive and inflexible, begin in adolescence or early adulthood, are stable over time, and lead to distress or impairment. They fall into three clusters:

    Tap a blurred cell to reveal it.
    ClusterThemeDisorders
    Cluster AOdd or eccentricParanoid, schizoid and schizotypal personality disorders.
    Cluster BDramatic, emotional or erraticAntisocial, histrionic, narcissistic and borderline personality disorders.
    Cluster CAnxious or fearfulAvoidant, dependent and obsessive-compulsive personality disorders.
    A, B, C

    A for Awkward (odd). B for Bold (dramatic). C for Cautious (anxious).

    Possible causes: biological, genetic, social, cultural, behavioral or cognitive sources.

    Part 7

    Common mix-ups

    Schizophrenia

    Loss of contact with reality: hallucinations, delusions.

    Dissociative identity disorder

    Two or more distinct identities.
    "Split personality" is DID, not schizophrenia.

    Bipolar I

    Full mania.

    Bipolar II

    Hypomania plus major depression.
    I = intense mania; II = milder highs, but real depression.

    Major depressive disorder

    Severe episodes.

    Persistent depressive disorder

    Milder but lasting at least two years.
    Severity versus duration.

    Agoraphobia

    Fear of places where escape is hard.

    Social anxiety disorder

    Fear of being judged.
    Ask what the person fears: being trapped, or being evaluated?

    OCD

    Obsessions and compulsions.

    Obsessive-compulsive personality disorder (Cluster C)

    A rigid, perfectionistic personality style.
    OCD has intrusive thoughts and rituals; the personality disorder is a lasting pattern of rigidity.

    Panic disorder

    Sudden, unexpected attacks.

    Generalized anxiety disorder

    Constant, nonspecific worry.
    A spike versus a steady background hum.

    What you need to be able to do

    Here is the good news to end the course on: psychological disorders are treatable, and therapy works.     How this shows up on the exam

    Expect a scenario asking which therapy, or which technique, a therapist is using. Match each technique to its perspective: free association (psychodynamic), cognitive restructuring (cognitive), systematic desensitization (behavioral), unconditional positive regard (humanistic).

    Part 1

    Research, trends and ethics

    Researchers who have run meta-analyses (studies that combine the results of many studies) of psychotherapy conclude that psychotherapies are generally effective. Many psychologists use evidence-based interventions: treatments supported by research.

    Therapeutic alliance

    A relationship of trust and shared goals between therapist and client. It is one of the strongest predictors of successful therapy.

    A client feels genuinely heard and works with the therapist on goals they agreed together.

    Cultural humility

    A therapist's openness to learning about, and respecting, the client's culture, rather than assuming their own view is the norm.

    A therapist asks how the client's family and faith shape the way they see their difficulties.

    From asylums to communities

    As psychotropic medications became more widely used and more effective, hospitals and asylums deinstitutionalized huge numbers of people in the late 20th century. Today, treatment is decentralized (in communities and clinics rather than large institutions), often combining medication with psychological therapy.

    Part 2

    Psychological therapies

    Tap a blurred cell to reveal it.
    ApproachGoalKey techniques
    PsychodynamicUncover the unconscious mind and bring hidden conflicts into awareness.Free association (saying whatever comes to mind) and dream interpretation.
    CognitiveChange maladaptive thinking.Cognitive restructuring (identifying and challenging distorted thoughts) and fear hierarchies. Focuses on the cognitive triad.
    Behavioral (applied behavior analysis)Use the principles of conditioning to change behavior.Exposure therapies such as systematic desensitization, aversion therapy, token economies and biofeedback.
    Cognitive-behavioral (CBT)Combine cognitive and behavioral techniques.Dialectical behavior therapy (DBT) and rational emotive behavior therapy (REBT).
    Humanistic (person-centered)Help the client grow in a supportive, accepting relationship.Active listening and unconditional positive regard.

    The cognitive triad

    Cognitive therapy proposes that people with depression tend to hold negative thoughts about three things: themselves, the world and the future. Therapy targets all three.

    Behavioral techniques up close

    Tap a blurred cell to reveal it.
    TechniqueHow it worksExample
    Systematic desensitizationGradual exposure to a fear, step by step up a fear hierarchy, while staying relaxed. Relaxation replaces fear (counterconditioning).Someone afraid of flying first looks at photos of planes, then visits an airport, then sits in a plane on the ground.
    Aversion therapyPairing an unwanted behavior with something unpleasant, so the behavior becomes linked with discomfort.Painting bitter-tasting polish on nails to stop nail biting.
    Token economyEarning tokens for desired behaviors that can be exchanged for rewards (operant conditioning).In a residential program, patients earn points for making their beds and attending sessions, then trade them for privileges.
    BiofeedbackUsing monitors to show body signals (heart rate, muscle tension) so the person learns to control them, helping regulate the sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous systems.Watching a heart rate monitor while practicing slow breathing to calm anxiety.

    Group and individual therapy

    In individual therapy, one client works one-to-one with a therapist, with full attention and privacy. In group therapy, a therapist works with several clients at once. Group therapy costs less per person, shows people that others share their problems, and lets members give and receive support and feedback. Family therapy treats the family as a system, and self-help or support groups bring together people facing the same challenge.

    Hypnosis

    Hypnosis has been shown to be effective in treating pain and anxiety. Research does not support using it to retrieve accurate memories or to "regress" people to an earlier age: hypnotized people can confidently "remember" things that never happened.

    Part 3

    Biological treatments

    Biological treatments target possible biochemical or physical causes of disorders.

    Tap a blurred cell to reveal it.
    TreatmentWhat it isUsed for
    AntidepressantsMedications that interact with neurotransmitters such as serotonin.Depression, and often anxiety.
    Antianxiety drugsMedications that reduce feelings of anxiety and tension.Anxiety disorders.
    LithiumA mood-stabilizing medication.Bipolar disorders.
    AntipsychoticsMedications that reduce dopamine activity.Schizophrenia (especially positive symptoms).

    Psychoactive medications can have side effects. For example, long-term use of some antipsychotics can cause tardive dyskinesia, a movement disorder (involuntary movements, often of the face and tongue) related to the regulation of dopamine.

    Tap a blurred cell to reveal it.
    InterventionWhat it involves
    PsychosurgerySurgery that removes or destroys (lesions) brain tissue to change behavior. The lobotomy was popular in the mid-20th century but is rarely, if ever, performed today.
    Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS)Magnetic pulses from a coil held near the scalp stimulate brain activity; it is noninvasive.
    Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT)A brief electric current is passed through the brain under anesthesia, causing a short seizure. Used mainly for severe depression that hasn't responded to other treatments.

    Common mix-ups

    Systematic desensitization

    Gradual exposure while relaxed.

    Aversion therapy

    Pairing a behavior with something unpleasant.
    One removes a fear; the other creates a dislike.

    Cognitive therapy

    Change the thoughts.

    Behavioral therapy

    Change the behavior through conditioning.
    CBT combines both.

    Psychodynamic therapy

    Uncover the unconscious; the therapist interprets.

    Person-centered therapy

    Active listening; the client leads.
    Who's in charge of the meaning: the analyst or the client?

    TMS

    Magnetic pulses; no seizure.

    ECT

    Electric current; brief seizure under anesthesia.
    Magnets versus electricity.

    Hypnosis: supported

    Pain and anxiety.

    Hypnosis: not supported

    Recovering memories, age regression.
