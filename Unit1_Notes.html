    Start here

    What you need to be able to do

    Why are you the way you are? Your genes had a say. So did your family, your friends, your school and the place you grew up. This topic is about how psychologists think about those two sets of influences, and how they try to separate them.

    By the end of this page, you should be able to:

    • Explain heredity (nature) and environment (nurture), and why psychologists say they interact rather than compete.
    • Explain the evolutionary perspective and natural selection, and why eugenics is a misuse of those ideas.
    • Explain what twin studies, family studies and adoption studies can (and cannot) tell us about genes and behavior.
    How this shows up on the exam

    Expect a short study description followed by "Which research design is this?" or "What does this finding suggest?" Your job is to spot the design (twin, family or adoption) and explain the logic: who shares genes, who shares an environment, and what a similarity or difference between them points to.

    Not on the exam

    Detailed genetics: genotype, phenotype, DNA, chromosomes, and dominant or recessive genes. You only need the psychology of heredity, not the biology class version.

    Part 1

    Nature and nurture

    Heredity ("nature")

    The genetic or predisposed characteristics you inherit, which influence your physical traits, behavior and mental processes.

    What you were born with.

    Environment ("nurture")

    The external factors you experience: family interactions, education, friends, culture, the places you live.

    What happens to you after that.

    Not "versus", but "and"

    The old framing was nature versus nurture, as if one side had to win. Psychologists now treat it as nature and nurture: the two interact to shape almost everything about us.

    Here's the thing. Asking which one matters more is a bit like asking whether a photo's quality comes from the camera or from the light. Change either one and the picture changes. Height is strongly influenced by genes, yet a child who is poorly fed will not reach the height their genes allowed. A teenager may inherit a quick musical ear, but without lessons, practice and a reason to play, that ear never becomes a skill.

    Going further (useful, but not named in the CED)

    Epigenetics shows how deep the interaction goes. Your environment and experiences can influence which genes are switched on or off, without changing the DNA itself. It is one reason identical twins can grow less alike over a lifetime.

    Part 2

    The evolutionary perspective

    The evolutionary perspective asks: how might this behavior have helped our ancestors survive and reproduce? It draws on Charles Darwin's idea of natural selection. Traits that helped individuals survive and have children were more likely to be passed on; traits that did not tended to fade out over many generations.

    Psychologists use this lens to explain tendencies that show up across cultures:

    • We tend to love sweet and fatty foods, which were rare and valuable sources of energy for our ancestors.
    • We learn to fear snakes, heights and spiders far more quickly than we learn to fear cars or electrical sockets, even though modern dangers are often deadlier.

    Notice the word tendencies. The evolutionary perspective explains why some behaviors come easily to humans. It does not mean those behaviors are fixed, right or unchangeable.

    Eugenics: a misuse of evolutionary ideas

    Eugenics was the belief that a population could be "improved" by encouraging some people to have children and preventing others. In the twentieth century it was used to justify forced sterilization and discrimination against groups labeled "unfit". The CED includes it as a warning: scientific ideas about heredity have been twisted to discriminate against people, and psychology has a responsibility not to repeat that.

    Keep these apart

    Natural selection describes how traits spread over generations. Eugenics was a human policy that tried to control who could reproduce. If a question describes a government or group deciding whose children are "desirable", the answer is eugenics.

    Part 3

    How researchers separate genes from environment

    You cannot ethically assign children to different families just to see what happens. So researchers use situations that already exist and compare people who share different amounts of genes and environment.

    Twin studies

    Compare identical twins (who share virtually all their genes) with fraternal twins (who share about half, like ordinary siblings). If identical twins are more alike on a trait, genes probably play a role.

    The strongest version: identical twins raised apart.

    Family studies

    Check whether a trait runs in families, and whether closer relatives are more alike than distant ones.

    Weakness: families share homes as well as genes.

    Adoption studies

    Compare adopted children with their biological parents (shared genes) and their adoptive parents (shared environment).

    Which family do they end up resembling?

    Classic trap: "proves it is genetic"

    These studies estimate how much genes contribute to differences between people. They never show that a trait is purely genetic, and they cannot tell you how much of one particular person's personality came from their genes.

    Common mix-ups

    Identical twins

    Share virtually all their genes.

    Fraternal twins

    Share about half their genes, the same as any brother or sister.
    The comparison between the two is what makes a twin study work.

    Family study

    Relatives share genes and usually a home.

    Adoption study

    Separates the people who gave the genes from the people who gave the environment.
    If a question asks which design best separates genes from environment, a family study is rarely the answer.

    Evolutionary perspective

    Explains why some behaviors may have helped our ancestors survive and reproduce.

    Eugenics

    A discriminatory movement that tried to control who could have children.
    One is a way of explaining behavior; the other is a misuse of it.

    Check yourself

    Answer each one in your head (or out loud) before you open it.

    1. Identical twins raised in different countries turn out to be very similar in how anxious they are. Fraternal twins raised together are much less similar. What does this suggest?
    A genetic contribution to anxiety. The identical twins share their genes but not their environment, yet they are still alike. The fraternal twins share an environment but only about half their genes, and they are less alike.
    2. Children who love reading tend to have parents who love reading. Why can't this family study tell us whether the love of reading is genetic?
    Because families share both genes and environment. These children got their parents' genes, but they also grew up in a home full of books and adults who read. A family study cannot separate the two.
    3. Adopted children's interest in sport turns out to match their adoptive parents' interest far more than their biological parents'. What does this point to?
    An environmental (nurture) influence. The adoptive parents supplied the environment but not the genes, so a stronger match with them points toward the environment shaping this interest.
    4. People in every culture studied learn to fear snakes much faster than they learn to fear flowers. Which perspective best explains this?
    The evolutionary perspective. Ancestors who were quick to fear snakes were more likely to survive and have children, so a readiness to learn that fear may have been passed on through natural selection.
    5. A historical policy allowed some citizens to have children while forcing others not to, based on their supposed "inherited quality". What is this, and why does the CED include it?
    Eugenics. It is included as an example of evolutionary and hereditary ideas being misused to discriminate against people.
    6. A friend says, "Math runs in my family's genes, and I didn't get them, so studying won't help." Using this topic, how would you reply?
    Nature and nurture interact. Genes may influence how easily something comes to you, but practice, teaching and effort (the environment) shape how far that ability actually goes. A genetic influence is not a fixed limit.

    What you need to be able to do

    Think about the minutes before a big exam. Nobody tells your heart to race or your mouth to go dry, yet both happen. Meanwhile you are choosing, on purpose, to pick up a pen. Two very different jobs, both handled by your nervous system. This topic is the map of how that system is organized.

    By the end of this page, you should be able to:

    • Tell the central nervous system (CNS) and peripheral nervous system (PNS) apart, and say what belongs to each.
    • Explain the difference between the somatic (voluntary) and autonomic (involuntary) nervous systems.
    • Predict what the sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions do to the body in a given situation.
    How this shows up on the exam

    You will usually get a person in a situation (a scare, a race, a calm evening) and be asked which division of the nervous system is most active. The most common mistake is swapping what the sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions do to heart rate, pupils and digestion, so learn that table cold.

    Part 1

    The map of the nervous system

    Tap any box to see what it does. Each split answers one question: is it in the brain and spinal cord or not? Is it voluntary or not? Is it speeding you up or calming you down?

    Tap a box in the diagram, or use the buttons.

    The nervous system

    Your body's communication network. It splits into two big parts: the central nervous system, which makes decisions, and the peripheral nervous system, which carries messages between the central system and the rest of your body.

    Central nervous system (CNS)

    The brain and spinal cord. It takes in information, processes it and sends out instructions, and it interacts with every process in the body. If it is inside your skull or spine, it is CNS.

    Brain

    The main control center for thinking, feeling, memory and voluntary movement. Topic 1.4 takes you through its parts.

    Spinal cord

    The information highway between the brain and the body. It also handles simple reflexes on its own, like pulling your hand from something hot (the reflex arc in Topic 1.3). The spinal cord is part of the CNS, not the PNS.

    Peripheral nervous system (PNS)

    All the nerves outside the brain and spinal cord. It relays messages from the CNS out to the muscles and organs, and brings information from the body back in. It has two branches: somatic and autonomic.

    Somatic nervous system

    Controls voluntary movement of your skeletal muscles: raising your hand, typing an answer, kicking a ball. It also carries sensory information from your skin and muscles back to the CNS.

    Hook: Somatic = skeletal muscles, by choice.

    Autonomic nervous system

    Controls involuntary processes that keep you alive without you thinking about them: heartbeat, breathing, digestion, sweating. It has two divisions that work in opposite directions: sympathetic and parasympathetic.

    Hook: Autonomic = automatic.

    Sympathetic division

    Arouses the body for action when you face a threat, a challenge or stress: the fight-or-flight response. Heart rate and breathing speed up, pupils widen, digestion slows down, and stored energy is released. It works alongside the hormone adrenaline (Topic 1.3).

    Hook: Sympathetic = sprint.

    Parasympathetic division

    Calms the body once the challenge has passed and helps it conserve energy: rest and digest. Heart rate and breathing slow down, pupils narrow, and digestion starts up again.

    Hook: Parasympathetic = pause.

    Part 2

    Sympathetic versus parasympathetic

    Picture Fatima on the morning of her AP exam. As she walks into the hall, her heart is pounding, her breathing is quick, her mouth is dry and her stomach feels like it has stopped working. That is her sympathetic division at work. Two hours later, the exam is over, she sits down with her friends and a snack, and everything slows down again. That is the parasympathetic division taking over.

    Common mix-ups

    Spinal cord

    Part of the central nervous system.

    Nerves branching off it

    Part of the peripheral nervous system.
    The spinal cord is not "peripheral" just because it runs down your back. Brain and spinal cord together are the CNS.

    Somatic

    Voluntary: skeletal muscles you choose to move.

    Autonomic

    Involuntary: organs and glands that run on their own.
    Typing your answer is somatic. The heart pounding while you type is autonomic.

    Sympathetic

    Speeds you up for action.

    Parasympathetic

    Calms you down to rest and digest.
    "Sympathetic" has nothing to do with feeling sympathy. Don't let the everyday meaning of the word fool you.

    What you need to be able to do

    Every thought you have had today, every scroll, every flinch at a sudden noise, started with a cell deciding whether or not to fire. This topic is about that decision: how a neuron fires, how it passes the message on, and what happens when chemicals (the body's own, or ones we take in) get involved.

    By the end of this page, you should be able to:

    • Explain what neurons and glial cells do, and how three types of neurons work together in a reflex arc.
    • Walk through neural firing step by step, and explain what goes wrong in multiple sclerosis and myasthenia gravis.
    • Match each of the eight listed neurotransmitters and five listed hormones to what it does.
    • Explain how drugs change neural communication (agonists, antagonists, reuptake inhibitors) and what each drug category does to neural activity.
    How this shows up on the exam

    The multiple-choice questions for this unit focus on functions, not structures. You will not be asked to label the parts of a neuron. You will be given a short scenario (a person, a symptom, a medication) and asked which process, neurotransmitter or drug action explains it. So for every term below, ask yourself: "What would I see if this worked too much, too little, or not at all?"

    Not on the exam

    The sodium-potassium pump; any neurotransmitter or hormone beyond the lists on this page; details about endocrine glands (the pituitary gland comes up in 1.4).

    Part 1

    Two kinds of cells

    Neurons: the messengers

    Neurons carry information. Inside the cell the message is electrical; between cells it becomes chemical. Every behavior and mental process you will study in this course is built on neurons talking to each other.

    Glial cells: the support crew

    Glial cells keep neurons working. They give structure, insulate (for example, the myelin around many axons), support communication and clear away waste. The neuron is the cell that carries the message itself.

    The reflex arc: your spinal cord acts first

    You grab the handle of a car door that has been sitting in the afternoon sun. Before you have even thought "hot", your hand has already let go. That is a reflex arc, and it needs three types of neurons:

    1 · In

    Sensory neuron

    Carries the "this is burning" signal from your skin to the spinal cord.

    2 · Middle

    Interneuron

    Sits inside the spinal cord (part of the central nervous system) and passes the signal straight across, while also sending it up to the brain.

    3 · Out

    Motor neuron

    Carries the command to your hand muscles: let go.

    The pain arrives a moment later, once your brain catches up. Move first, feel second: that order is what makes reflexes protective. It is also a nice example of the central and peripheral nervous systems (Topic 1.2) working together.

    Memory hook

    S-I-M: Sensory brings it in, the Interneuron sits in the middle, the Motor neuron sends it out to the muscles.

    Part 2

    How a neuron fires

    Neural firing happens in the same orderly sequence every time. Step through it below and watch where you are on the graph of the neuron's charge.

    Step 1 of 7

    Resting potential: charged and ready

    At rest, the inside of the neuron is slightly negative compared with the outside (about -70 millivolts). Resting does not mean switched off. The neuron is charged and ready to go, like a phone on standby.

    Step 2 of 7

    Messages arrive and add up

    Other neurons send signals to this neuron's dendrites. Excitatory messages nudge the charge up toward firing. Inhibitory messages push it down, away from firing. The neuron is constantly adding these up.

    Step 3 of 7

    Threshold: fire or don't

    If the total reaches the threshold (about -55 millivolts), the neuron fires. If it falls short, nothing happens. This is the all-or-none principle: a neuron never fires halfway, and a stronger push does not make a bigger impulse.

    Step 4 of 7

    Depolarization: the action potential

    Positively charged ions rush into the cell, and the charge flips from negative to positive. This electrical impulse, the action potential, travels down the axon toward the terminal. Myelin, the insulation made by glial cells, helps it travel faster.

    Step 5 of 7

    Refractory period: recharging

    Right after firing, the neuron resets back to its resting charge. During this short refractory period it cannot fire again, a bit like a camera flash that needs a second to recharge before the next photo.

    Step 6 of 7

    The message turns chemical

    At the axon terminal, the electrical signal triggers the release of neurotransmitters into the synapse, the tiny gap between two neurons. They cross the gap and lock onto receptor sites on the receiving neuron, sending it an excitatory or inhibitory message (back to Step 2 for the next neuron).

    Step 7 of 7

    Cleanup: reuptake

    Leftover neurotransmitters do not hang around in the gap. Some are broken down, and many are pulled back into the sending neuron to be reused. That recycling is called reuptake. Keep it in mind: several drugs work by interfering with exactly this step.

    Classic trap: "a stronger stimulus makes a bigger spike"

    A harder pinch does not make each action potential bigger. Every action potential is the same size. Your brain reads intensity from how often neurons fire and how many fire at once. Think of a doorbell, not a volume knob: an urgent visitor rings more often, not louder.

    When transmission goes wrong

    Part 6

    How psychoactive drugs change the message

    Psychoactive drugs change behavior and mental processes by interfering with neural communication. The CED names specific drugs as examples, so the exam uses them. What you are learning here is what each one does to neural activity.

    Three ways a drug can act

    Think of a neurotransmitter as a key and a receptor site as a lock.

    Agonist

    Mimics or boosts a neurotransmitter, so its effect gets stronger. Opioids, for example, act like endorphins at their receptors.

    A copied key that also opens the lock.

    Antagonist

    Blocks the receptor, so the real neurotransmitter cannot deliver its message. A drug that blocks acetylcholine receptors at the muscles would cause weakness.

    Gum stuck in the keyhole.

    Reuptake inhibitor

    Stops the sending neuron from pulling the neurotransmitter back (Step 7), so more stays in the synapse for longer. SSRI antidepressants do this with serotonin; cocaine does it with dopamine.

    Someone blocking the recycling bin.

    Four drug categories

    CategoryCED examplesEffect on neural activityWhat you might observe
    StimulantsCaffeine, cocaineIncrease neural activityMore alert and energetic; can bring restlessness, anxiety and trouble sleeping.
    DepressantsAlcoholDecrease neural activitySlower reactions, poorer judgment and memory, drowsiness.
    HallucinogensMarijuanaDistort perception and/or thinkingChanges in how things look, sound or feel; altered sense of time.
    OpioidsHeroinRelieve painPain relief and drowsiness; a very high risk of addiction.

    Tolerance, addiction and withdrawal

    These three describe how repeated drug use changes the brain over time, and they usually come as a chain:

    • Tolerance: the brain adapts to the drug, so the same dose has less effect and more is needed to feel the same thing.
    • Addiction: compulsive use that continues despite harm to the person's health, relationships or responsibilities.
    • Withdrawal: the unpleasant (sometimes dangerous) physical and psychological symptoms that appear when a person with an addiction stops using the drug.

    Part 8

    Check yourself

    Answer each one in your head (or out loud) before you open it.

    1. Rania touches a scorching car door handle and lets go before she feels any pain. Which neuron passes the signal across inside her spinal cord?
    The interneuron. The sensory neuron brings the signal in, the interneuron in the spinal cord passes it straight to the motor neuron, and the motor neuron tells her hand to let go. The brain hears about it a moment later.
    2. Yousef stubs his toe hard, and it hurts far more than a light bump. Did his neurons fire bigger action potentials?
    No. By the all-or-none principle, every action potential is the same size. The harder knock made more neurons fire, and fire more often, and that is what his brain read as "this really hurts".
    3. A medication stops the sending neuron from reabsorbing serotonin. What kind of drug action is this, and what happens in the synapse?
    A reuptake inhibitor. More serotonin stays in the synapse for longer, so its message keeps being received. This is how SSRI antidepressants are designed to work.
    4. A toxin blocks the acetylcholine receptors on muscle cells. Agonist or antagonist? Predict what the person would experience.
    An antagonist. Acetylcholine can no longer deliver its message to the muscles, so you would expect muscle weakness or, at high doses, paralysis. Notice that this looks a lot like myasthenia gravis, where the receptors are blocked by the immune system instead.
    5. Mariam skipped breakfast. By 11 a.m. her stomach is growling and she cannot focus in class. Which hormone is rising?
    Ghrelin. It is released when the stomach is empty and increases hunger. After lunch, leptin and a full stomach help switch the hunger signal off.
    6. A drug strengthens the effect of GABA. Would you expect the person to become calmer or more alert?
    Calmer. GABA is the main inhibitory neurotransmitter, so a GABA agonist makes firing less likely: slower reactions, relaxation, drowsiness. This is the same direction as depressants such as alcohol, which boost GABA's effect.

    Part 1

    The lobes of the cortex, and what lies below

    The cerebral cortex is the wrinkled outer layer of the brain, split into two hemispheres and four lobes on each side. Tap any part of the diagram to see what it does.

    Left hemisphere seen from the side. A simplified sketch, not to scale.

    Frontal lobes

    Just behind your forehead. They handle higher-order thinking, executive functioning (planning, decision-making, self-control) and language processing. They contain the prefrontal cortex, the motor cortex and Broca's area.

    Case study link: after an iron rod passed through Phineas Gage's frontal lobe in 1848, he survived, but people who knew him said his personality and self-control changed dramatically.

    Prefrontal cortex

    The very front part of the frontal lobes. It is the center of executive functioning: planning ahead, weighing consequences, controlling impulses, making judgments. It is one of the last brain areas to finish maturing, continuing into the mid-twenties.

    Motor cortex

    A strip at the rear of the frontal lobes that controls most voluntary skeletal movement. It sends commands out, so stimulating it makes a body part move. It works contralaterally: the left motor cortex moves the right side of the body.

    Broca's area

    Usually in the left frontal lobe. Responsible for speech production. Damage causes a type of aphasia in which the person mostly understands language but speaks slowly, with great effort, in broken phrases.

    Parietal lobes

    Near the back of the crown of your head. They contain the somatosensory cortex (touch) and association areas that process and organize information, for example combining what you see and feel to judge where things are in space.

    Somatosensory cortex

    A strip at the front of the parietal lobes, right behind the motor cortex. It processes touch sensitivity: pressure, temperature, pain. It takes information in, so stimulating it makes a person feel a touch. Sensitive areas like the lips and fingertips get the most cortex.

    Temporal lobes

    On the sides of the brain, near your ears (think "temples"). They handle auditory processing (hearing) and linguistic processing, including understanding speech in Wernicke's area. The hippocampus sits on the inside of each temporal lobe.

    Wernicke's area

    Usually in the left temporal lobe. Responsible for speech comprehension. Damage causes a type of aphasia in which speech flows easily but makes little sense, and the person struggles to understand others.

    Occipital lobes

    At the back of the brain. They process visual information. Damage here can cause visual problems even when the eyes are healthy, including face blindness and blindsight (Topic 1.6).

    Cerebellum

    The "little brain" at the back, below the occipital lobes. It coordinates muscle movement, maintains balance and supports some procedural learning, like riding a bike or touch-typing. Alcohol disrupts it, which is one reason a drunk person struggles to walk in a straight line.

    Brain stem

    Where the brain meets the spinal cord. It controls basic life functions. Its lowest part, the medulla, regulates breathing and heart rate. The reticular activating system runs through it too (see the table below). Serious damage here is often fatal.

    Memory hooks

    Occipital sounds like "optical": vision at the back. Temporal sits by your temples and ears: hearing. Parietal on top: "pat" the top of your head to feel touch. Frontal at the front: plans what you do next. And the motor and sensory strips sit side by side: motor in front, sensation behind.

    Part 3

    Two hemispheres, one brain

    Contralateral organization

    Each hemisphere mainly controls and senses the opposite side of the body. A stroke in the left hemisphere usually affects movement on the right side of the body. Vision works the same way by visual field: what you see on your right side is processed by your left hemisphere.

    Split-brain research

    For some people with severe epilepsy, surgeons cut the corpus callosum to stop seizures spreading from one side of the brain to the other. Afterwards, the two hemispheres can no longer share information directly, which let researchers test what each side does on its own.

    A split-brain patient is shown a word...It goes to...What happens
    In the right visual fieldThe left hemisphereThey can say the word out loud, because language is usually in the left hemisphere.
    In the left visual fieldThe right hemisphereThey usually cannot say it, but their left hand (controlled by the right hemisphere) can pick out the matching object.

    Findings like these show that the hemispheres may specialize. Language areas (Broca's and Wernicke's) are typically in the left hemisphere, while the right tends to contribute more to tasks like recognizing faces and reading spatial layouts.

    Classic trap: "left-brained and right-brained people"

    Specialization does not mean some people are logical "left-brainers" and others creative "right-brainers". In a healthy brain the hemispheres constantly work together through the corpus callosum. That popular idea is a myth.

    Brain plasticity

    Plasticity is the brain's ability to rewire itself, change existing connections and create new ones throughout life. It allows other areas to take over some of the work of a damaged area, which is why stroke patients can regain abilities with therapy. Plasticity is strongest in childhood: children who have had an entire hemisphere removed to stop severe seizures can recover a surprising amount of function.

    What you need to be able to do

    You will spend roughly a third of your life asleep. That is not wasted time: your brain is busy all night, cycling through stages that look nothing alike. This topic covers what happens across a night, why we sleep and dream, and what happens when sleep goes wrong. (It also explains why the night before an exam is the worst time to give up sleep.)

    By the end of this page, you should be able to:

    • Explain consciousness, the circadian rhythm, and how jet lag and shift work disrupt it.
    • Describe the stages of sleep by their EEG patterns, and how the sleep cycle changes across the night.
    • Explain the two theories of dreaming and the two theories of why we sleep.
    • Identify the five sleep disorders in the CED from a description.
    How this shows up on the exam

    Expect EEG descriptions ("fast, irregular waves, but the muscles are completely relaxed") and sleep scenarios ("a child walks to the kitchen at midnight and remembers nothing"). The most common errors: placing sleepwalking in REM (it happens in deep NREM sleep), and forgetting that REM, not Stage 3, is the stage linked with dreaming.

    Not on the exam

    The psychoanalytic (Freudian) theory of dreams, and any sleep disorder beyond the five on this page.

    Part 1

    Consciousness and your body clock

    Consciousness is your awareness of your thoughts, feelings, behavior and what is happening inside and around you. It is not simply on or off: it has levels. Sleep and wakefulness are two different types of consciousness. Even asleep, your brain still notices some things, which is why you can sleep through traffic but wake up when someone says your name.

    The circadian rhythm

    Your sleep/wake cycle follows a circadian rhythm, an internal body clock that runs on roughly a 24-hour cycle. Light is its main signal: as it gets dark, the hormone melatonin rises (Topic 1.3) and you start to feel sleepy.

    Jet lag

    Fly from Dubai to New York and your body clock still thinks it is Dubai time. You are wide awake at 3 a.m. and exhausted at lunch, until your rhythm catches up with the new time zone.

    Shift work

    Nurses, pilots and security staff who work through the night ask their bodies to be awake when the circadian rhythm says sleep. Over time this can harm alertness, mood and health.

    Part 2

    A night of sleep, stage by stage

    Researchers tell the stages of sleep apart by their EEG patterns, the shape of the brain waves recorded from the scalp. You move through a full cycle of stages about every 90 minutes, four to six times a night. Tap a stage to see its brain waves and what is going on.

    Awake

    Alertfast, small waves (beta)
    Relaxed, eyes closedslower, regular waves (alpha)

    When you are alert and thinking, brain waves are fast and small. As you relax and get drowsy, they slow into a more regular rhythm.

    Stage 1 (NREM): drifting off

    Stage 1slower, irregular waves (theta)

    A short, light transition into sleep, lasting only a few minutes. You are easy to wake and may insist you were not asleep at all. As you enter it, you may have hypnagogic sensations: the feeling of falling, a sudden jerk, or brief dream-like images.

    Stage 2 (NREM): true sleep

    Stage 2sleep spindles and K-complexes

    Now you are clearly asleep and harder to wake. The EEG shows sleep spindles (short bursts of fast activity) and K-complexes (single large waves). You spend more of the night in Stage 2 than in any other stage.

    Stage 3 (NREM): deep sleep

    Stage 3slow, large waves (delta)

    The deepest sleep, with slow, large delta waves. You are very hard to wake, and if someone does wake you, you feel groggy and confused. Look at the graph: Stage 3 is long early in the night and almost disappears by morning, because NREM stages get shorter as the night goes on. Sleepwalking and sleep talking happen here.

    REM sleep: paradoxical sleep

    REMfast waves, almost like being awake

    Your eyes dart around under your eyelids (Rapid Eye Movement). It is called paradoxical sleep because the brain waves look almost like being awake, yet the body is at its most relaxed: your major muscles are effectively paralyzed. This is when most dreaming happens. Notice on the graph that REM periods get longer as the night goes on, so the last hours of sleep are full of dreams.

    If you are deprived of REM sleep (say, after several short nights), you will enter REM faster and spend more time in it when you finally sleep properly. This is REM rebound.

    Two patterns to remember

    Deep sleep early, dreams late. Stage 3 shrinks across the night while REM periods grow. Cutting your sleep short in the morning mostly costs you REM.

    Part 3

    Why do we sleep? Why do we dream?

    These are two separate questions, and the CED gives you two theories for each. Keep them in separate boxes in your head.

    Why we sleep

    Restoration theory

    Sleep restores the body and brain, replenishing resources used up during the day and repairing tissue.

    Sleep as overnight charging.

    Memory consolidation theory

    Sleep helps the brain organize and strengthen (consolidate) the memories formed during the day.

    Sleep as saving your work.

    Why we dream

    Activation-synthesis theory

    During REM sleep the brain stem fires off random neural activity. The cortex tries to make sense of it by weaving it into a story: the dream.

    Dreams as the brain's best guess.

    Consolidation theory of dreams

    Dreams are part of how the brain sorts, processes and stores the day's experiences and memories.

    Dreams as the brain filing.

    Apply it to yourself

    Pulling an all-nighter before a test works against both "why we sleep" theories: your brain has had no time to restore itself, and no chance to consolidate what you studied. The research on sleep and memory is one of the strongest arguments for going to bed.

    Part 4

    Sleep disorders: the five you need

    Sleep problems affect physical and cognitive performance during the day: attention, memory, mood and reaction time. Treating sleep disorders and keeping a regular sleep schedule can improve both daytime performance and overall well-being.

    What you need to be able to do

    Your eyes do not see and your ears do not hear. They detect energy (light, vibrations, chemicals, pressure) and turn it into neural messages. Your brain does the rest. This topic is about that first step: how each sense picks up the world and hands it over to the brain.

    By the end of this page, you should be able to:

    • Explain transduction, absolute threshold, the just-noticeable difference, Weber's law, sensory adaptation, sensory interaction and synesthesia.
    • Explain how the eye works, including rods and cones, the blind spot, and the two theories of color vision.
    • Explain how hearing works, the three theories of pitch, sound localization and the two types of hearing loss.
    • Explain smell, taste, touch, pain, balance (vestibular sense) and body movement (kinesthesis).
    How this shows up on the exam

    Sensation is one of the most-missed topics in Unit 1, mostly because of a few confusable pairs: absolute threshold versus difference threshold, rods versus cones, trichromatic versus opponent-process theory, place versus frequency theory, conduction versus sensorineural hearing loss. Expect a short scenario and a question asking which concept explains it. The mix-ups section collects all of them.

    Part 1

    From the world to the brain

    Sensation is the process of detecting information from the environment and transducing it, which means converting it into neural messages the brain can process. What the brain then does with those messages (recognizing, organizing, interpreting) is perception, which you will meet in Unit 2.

    ConceptWhat it meansEveryday example
    Absolute thresholdThe weakest stimulus you can detect at least 50 percent of the time.The faintest phone vibration you notice about half the time it buzzes.
    Just-noticeable difference (difference threshold)The smallest change in a stimulus you can detect at least half the time.How much sugar has to be added to your karak before you notice it is sweeter.
    Weber's lawThe just-noticeable difference is a constant proportion of the original stimulus, not a fixed amount.Adding one candle to two is obvious; adding one to fifty is not.

    Try it: Weber's law and your school bag

    For lifted weight, the just-noticeable difference is roughly 2 percent of the starting weight. Move the slider to change how heavy your bag already is.

    The heavier the bag, the bigger the change needs to be. That is Weber's law: the change you notice depends on the proportion, not the amount. (Other senses have their own percentages; 2 percent is an approximate figure used for illustration.)

    Part 2

    Vision

    Getting light onto the retina

    Light enters the eye and the lens focuses it onto the retina, the light-sensitive surface at the back of the eye. The lens changes shape to focus on near or far objects, a process called accommodation. When the focusing goes wrong:

    • Nearsightedness: the image focuses in front of the retina, so distant objects look blurry (you can see near things clearly).
    • Farsightedness: the image focuses behind the retina, so nearby objects look blurry (you can see far things clearly).

    Rods and cones

    Cells in the retina capture visual information and transduce it into neural messages.

    Tap a blurred cell to reveal it.
    RodsCones
    WhereThe periphery (edges) of the retinaConcentrated in the fovea, the center of the retina
    DetectShapes and movement, but not colorColor and fine detail
    Work best inLow lightBright light

    Memory hook

    Cones see Color in the Center. Rods take over in the dark, which is why everything looks gray on a night-time walk.

    The blind spot

    Where the visual (optic) nerve leaves the eye there are no receptor cells, so each eye has a blind spot. You never notice it, because your brain fills in the gap to give you a complete-looking picture. That is evidence the retina sends the brain an incomplete image.

    Try it: find your blind spot

    Close your left eye. With your right eye, stare at the +. Slowly move your face toward and away from the screen. At one distance the dot will vanish.

    +

    Seeing color: two theories, both right

    Color vision is explained by both theories. They describe different stages of processing.

    Trichromatic theory

    The retina has three types of cones, most sensitive to blue, green and red light. Every color you see comes from the combination of signals from those three.

    Three cones, mixing like a screen's pixels.

    Opponent-process theory

    Further along, ganglion cells in the retina work in opposing pairs: red/green, blue/yellow and black/white. When one side of a pair is activated, the other is not. This explains afterimages.

    Three pairs, each a tug of war.

    Try it: an afterimage

    Press start and stare at the small orange dot in the middle for 20 seconds without moving your eyes. The picture will then disappear.

    You should see the opposite colors: a reddish circle where the green was, a bluish square where the yellow was, and a lighter bar where the black was. The cells you tired out stopped firing, so their opponents took over for a moment.

    When vision goes wrong

    • Color vision deficiency: damage or irregularities in one or more types of cone or ganglion cell (most often red/green, sometimes blue/yellow). Dichromatism means seeing with only two working cone types; monochromatism means seeing no color at all, only shades of light and dark.
    • Prosopagnosia (face blindness): the person can see faces perfectly well but cannot recognize them, even a family member's.
    • Blindsight: the person says they cannot see objects in part of their visual field, yet can still respond to them, for example pointing to where something is or stepping around it.

    Both prosopagnosia and blindsight come from damage to the parts of the brain responsible for vision (mainly the occipital lobes, Topic 1.4), not from damage to the eyes.

    Part 3

    Hearing

    Sound is the movement of air molecules in waves. Two features of a sound wave matter here:

    • Wavelength determines pitch. Short wavelengths (high frequency) sound high, like a whistle. Long wavelengths (low frequency) sound low, like a bass drum.
    • Amplitude (the height of the wave) determines loudness.

    How do we hear pitch? Three theories

    TheoryThe ideaExplains best
    Place theoryDifferent places along the basilar membrane in the cochlea vibrate most for different pitches. The brain reads pitch from where the hair cells are being stimulated.High pitches
    Frequency theoryThe auditory nerve fires at the same rate as the sound wave's frequency. The brain reads pitch from how fast the nerve fires.Low pitches
    Volley theoryA single neuron cannot fire fast enough for mid-range pitches, so groups of neurons take turns firing, like a team of archers sending volleys of arrows. Together they match the frequency.Middle pitches, beyond what one neuron can manage
    Memory hook

    Place = where on the membrane. Frequency = how fast the nerve fires. Volley = taking turns to go faster than one neuron can.

    Where is that sound coming from?

    Sound localization is how we work out where a sound comes from. A sound on your right reaches your right ear slightly sooner and slightly louder than your left. Your brain uses those tiny differences in timing and loudness to place the sound. Sounds directly in front of you or behind you are the hardest to place, because they reach both ears at the same moment.

    Two kinds of hearing loss

    Conduction deafness

    Damage to the mechanical parts that conduct sound to the inner ear, such as the eardrum or the tiny bones of the middle ear. Sound simply does not get through well. Hearing aids that amplify sound often help.

    Sensorineural deafness

    Damage to the hair cells in the cochlea or to the auditory nerve, often from aging or long exposure to loud noise. Sound arrives, but it is not turned into neural messages properly. Cochlear implants can help some people.

    Spot the difference

    Conduction = the sound can't get in. Sensorineural = the sound gets in, but the sensory receptors or nerve can't pass it on. A teenager who plays music through earphones at full volume every day is risking sensorineural loss.

    Part 4

    The chemical senses: smell and taste

    Smell (olfaction)

    Receptors in the nose detect odor molecules and transduce them into neural messages. Smell is special in one way you need to know: it is the only sense not processed first in the thalamus. Smell signals travel more directly to brain areas linked with emotion and memory, which may be why a scent can bring back a memory so vividly. Pheromones are chemical messages, detected through the olfactory system, that animals of the same species use to signal to one another.

    Taste (gustation)

    Receptors on the tongue and in the mouth detect six basic tastes: sweet, sour, salty, bitter, umami (savory, like a rich broth) and oleogustus (the taste of fat). The number of taste receptors a person has affects how strongly they taste:

    • Supertasters have the most receptors and taste things intensely, especially bitter flavors like strong coffee or dark green vegetables.
    • Medium tasters are in between.
    • Nontasters have the fewest receptors and find flavors milder.

    Smell and taste work together

    What you call "taste" is mostly the two chemical senses interacting. Without smell, taste is muted or missing, which is why food seems flavorless when you have a blocked nose. This is a clear example of sensory interaction.

    Part 5

    Touch, pain, balance and movement

    Touch

    Receptors in the skin detect pressure, warmth, cold and pain, and the brain processes them (in the somatosensory cortex, Topic 1.4). One surprise the CED wants you to know: the sensation of "hot" is produced when warm and cold receptors are activated at the same time.

    Pain

    Pain is processed both in the body and in the brain, which is why the same injury can hurt more or less depending on what you are paying attention to.

    • Gate control theory proposes a neural "gate" in the spinal cord that can open to let pain signals through to the brain or close to block them. Rubbing a bumped elbow, or being absorbed in a match, can help close the gate.
    • Phantom limb sensation is when people who have lost a limb still feel sensations or pain where the limb used to be. It shows that pain can be produced by the brain even when the body part is gone.

    Balance and body movement

    Vestibular sense

    Your sense of balance. It is detected mainly by the fluid-filled semicircular canals in the inner ear, together with structures in the brain. Spin around quickly and stop: the fluid keeps moving, which is why the room seems to keep spinning.

    Kinesthesis

    Your sense of your own body movement and position. It lets you move in a coordinated way without looking, like touching your nose with your eyes closed or typing without looking at the keys.

    Part 6

    Common mix-ups

    Absolute threshold

    "Is it there?" The weakest stimulus you detect half the time.

    Difference threshold (JND)

    "Is it different?" The smallest change you detect half the time.
    If the scenario starts from nothing, it is absolute threshold. If something is already there and changes, it is difference threshold.

    Sensory adaptation

    Your senses stop responding to an unchanging stimulus.

    Weber's law

    The change you notice is a constant proportion of the original.
    Adaptation is about not noticing over time; Weber's law is about how big a change must be to notice.

    Trichromatic theory

    Three cone types (blue, green, red) combine to make every color.

    Opponent-process theory

    Opposing pairs of ganglion cells; explains afterimages.
    Afterimage in the question? Opponent-process. "Three types of cones"? Trichromatic. Both theories are correct.

    Place theory

    Pitch from where the basilar membrane vibrates; best for high pitches.

    Frequency theory

    Pitch from how fast the nerve fires; best for low pitches.
    Volley theory is frequency theory with teamwork: groups of neurons taking turns.

    Conduction deafness

    Eardrum or middle-ear bones damaged; sound cannot get in.

    Sensorineural deafness

    Hair cells or auditory nerve damaged; sound is not transduced.
    Loud noise and aging cause sensorineural loss.

    Vestibular sense

    Balance, via the semicircular canals.

    Kinesthesis

    Body movement and position, without looking.
    Dizzy after a spinning ride: vestibular. Touching your nose with your eyes closed: kinesthesis.

    Check yourself

    Answer each one in your head (or out loud) before you open it.

    1. In a hearing test, a tone gets quieter and quieter. The researcher records the volume at which Sara hears it on half of the trials. What has been measured?
    Sara's absolute threshold for that tone: the weakest stimulus she detects at least 50 percent of the time.
    2. Adding 1 kg to a 2 kg bag is easy to notice, but adding 1 kg to a 20 kg suitcase is not. Which principle explains this?
    Weber's law. The just-noticeable difference is a constant proportion of the original weight, so the heavier the starting weight, the bigger the change has to be.
    3. Walking into the desert at night, Yousef can make out the shapes of rocks but cannot tell what color anything is. Which cells is he relying on?
    Rods. They work in low light and detect shapes and movement, but not color. Cones need brighter light.
    4. After staring at a green circle for 30 seconds, Lina looks at a white wall and sees a reddish circle. Which theory explains this?
    Opponent-process theory. The green side of the red/green ganglion cells became fatigued, so when she looked away the red side briefly dominated.
    5. A retired factory worker can hear that people are speaking but struggles to make out the words. Tests show the hair cells in his cochlea are damaged. Which type of hearing loss is this?
    Sensorineural deafness, caused by damage to the hair cells (or auditory nerve), often from years of loud noise or aging.
    6. With a heavy cold, Hamad says his favorite biryani "tastes of nothing". Which concept explains this?
    Sensory interaction. Flavor depends on smell and taste working together, so with his sense of smell blocked, taste is muted.
    7. A patient who lost her right hand in an accident still feels it itching. What is this called?
    Phantom limb sensation. The brain continues to produce sensations from a body part that is no longer there.
