    What you need to be able to do

    A classmate walks past without saying hello. Is she rude, or did she just get bad news? We explain other people's behavior all day long, usually without noticing, and our explanations are often biased in predictable ways. This topic is about how we explain behavior and how we judge ourselves and others.

    By the end of this page, you should be able to:

    • Tell dispositional and situational attributions apart, and explain optimistic and pessimistic explanatory styles.
    • Explain the actor/observer bias, the fundamental attribution error and the self-serving bias.
    • Explain internal and external locus of control.
    • Explain the mere exposure effect, the self-fulfilling prophecy, social comparison (upward and downward) and relative deprivation.
    How this shows up on the exam

    The three attribution biases are a favorite. They have different targets: the fundamental attribution error is about judging others; the actor/observer bias contrasts how we explain ourselves versus others; the self-serving bias is about protecting our self-image.

    Part 1

    Explaining behavior

    An attribution is how we explain behavior, our own and other people's.

    Dispositional attribution

    Explaining behavior by internal qualities: personality, intelligence, effort.

    "She didn't say hello because she's rude."

    Situational attribution

    Explaining behavior by external circumstances.

    "She didn't say hello because she just got bad news."

    Explanatory style

    People show a predictable pattern in how they explain good and bad events: their explanatory style.

    Optimistic explanatory style

    Bad events are explained as temporary, specific and not entirely your fault; good events as lasting and earned.

    "I failed this quiz because I didn't review the right chapter. Next time I will."

    Pessimistic explanatory style

    Bad events are explained as permanent, affecting everything, and your fault.

    "I failed because I'm useless at everything, and I always will be."

    Biased explanations

    Tap a blurred cell to reveal it.
    BiasWhat it isExample
    Fundamental attribution errorOverestimating dispositional causes and underestimating situational ones when judging other people.A driver cuts you off: "What a selfish person." (Maybe they are rushing to the hospital.)
    Actor/observer biasExplaining your own behavior by the situation, but others' by their disposition."I was late because of traffic. He was late because he's disorganized."
    Self-serving biasTaking credit for successes (dispositional) and blaming failures on the situation, to protect self-esteem."I got an A because I'm smart. I got a C because the test was unfair."
    Part 2

    Locus of control

    Internal locus of control

    Believing you control what happens to you, through your own effort and choices.

    "If I revise properly, I'll do well."

    External locus of control

    Believing outside forces (luck, fate, other people) control what happens to you.

    "It doesn't matter how much I revise; it depends on which questions come up."

    People with a stronger internal locus of control tend to put in more effort and cope better with setbacks, because they believe their actions make a difference.

    Person perception

    Tap a blurred cell to reveal it.
    ConceptWhat it isExample
    Mere exposure effectBeing exposed to something repeatedly makes us like it more.A song you disliked at first grows on you after hearing it every day in the car.
    Self-fulfilling prophecyOur beliefs about someone lead us to act in ways that make those beliefs come true.A teacher who expects a student to do well gives her more attention and encouragement, and she does do well.
    Upward social comparisonComparing yourself with someone who seems better off.Comparing your grades with the top student in the year: motivating, or discouraging.
    Downward social comparisonComparing yourself with someone who seems worse off.Feeling better about your mark after hearing others found the test harder.

    Part 4

    Common mix-ups

    Fundamental attribution error

    Judging others by disposition, ignoring the situation.

    Actor/observer bias

    Me: the situation. Them: their disposition.
    If the scenario compares the person's own behavior with someone else's, it is actor/observer.

    Self-serving bias

    Success is mine; failure is the situation's.

    Explanatory style

    A general habit of explaining good and bad events.
    Self-serving bias is about protecting self-esteem in a single judgment.

    Internal locus of control

    "My actions decide what happens."

    External locus of control

    "Luck or others decide what happens."
    Listen for who the person credits: themselves, or outside forces.

    Upward comparison

    Comparing with those better off.

    Relative deprivation

    Feeling deprived because of comparison.
    Relative deprivation is often the result of upward comparison.

    What you need to be able to do

    We all carry mental shortcuts about groups of people. That is partly just how memory works: categories help us process the world faster. The problem starts when those categories harden into attitudes, and the attitudes harden into actions. This topic is about how that happens, and why attitudes are so hard to change.

    By the end of this page, you should be able to:

    • Explain stereotypes, prejudice and discrimination, and how they are connected.
    • Explain implicit attitudes, the just-world phenomenon, out-group homogeneity bias, in-group bias and ethnocentrism.
    • Explain belief perseverance, confirmation bias and cognitive dissonance.
    How this shows up on the exam

    Expect scenarios asking you to tell a belief (stereotype) from a feeling (prejudice) from an action (discrimination), and to spot the bias in a group judgment. A useful contrast: cognitive dissonance pushes attitudes to change, while belief perseverance resists change.

    Part 1

    Stereotypes, prejudice and discrimination

    Tap a blurred cell to reveal it.
    What it isExample
    StereotypeA belief (thought) about a group. Stereotypes reduce mental effort but can be biased, and they often lead to prejudice."Students from that school are all arrogant."
    PrejudiceA feeling, usually negative, toward a group.Disliking students from that school before meeting any of them.
    DiscriminationAn action toward someone because of their group.Refusing to partner with anyone from that school in a project.
    Think, feel, act

    Stereotype = what you think. Prejudice = what you feel. Discrimination = what you do.

    Implicit attitudes

    Implicit attitudes are attitudes we hold but may not be aware of, or may not admit, even to ourselves. Researchers have shown how they appear as negative judgments of others:

    Tap a blurred cell to reveal it.
    ConceptWhat it isExample
    Just-world phenomenonBelieving the world is fair, so people get what they deserve. It leads to blaming people for their own misfortune.A classmate leaves a demanding program, and others assume she simply didn't try hard enough, without knowing what she was dealing with.
    Out-group homogeneity biasSeeing members of other groups as all the same, while seeing your own group as varied."Fans of that football club are all the same," while your own club's fans are "all different kinds of people".
    In-group biasFavoring your own group.A referee's call seems fair when it helps your team and outrageous when it doesn't.
    EthnocentrismJudging other cultures by the standards of your own, as if yours is the normal or correct way.Assuming the way your family does things (shoes on or off indoors) is simply the right way.
    Part 2

    Why attitudes resist change

    When people meet evidence that contradicts a belief they care about, they often do not change the belief. They attack the evidence.

    Belief perseverance

    Holding on to a belief even after evidence shows it is wrong. It is often kept alive by confirmation bias: noticing and remembering only the information that supports it.

    A student insists rereading is the best way to study, even after seeing research that shows it is one of the least effective methods.

    Cognitive dissonance

    The mental discomfort we feel when our actions and attitudes conflict. To reduce it, we change either the action or the attitude so they line up again.

    A student who sees himself as honest copies homework under pressure. He either confesses (changes the action) or tells himself "it was just once, everyone does it" (changes the attitude).

    Change versus resist

    Cognitive dissonance is a force for attitude change (to match behavior). Belief perseverance is a force against change (despite evidence).

    Part 3

    Common mix-ups

    Prejudice

    A feeling toward a group.

    Discrimination

    An action toward a group.
    Prejudice can exist without discrimination; discrimination is the behavior.

    Out-group homogeneity bias

    "They're all the same."

    In-group bias

    "We're better."
    One is about seeing sameness; the other is about favoring your side.

    Belief perseverance

    Holding on to a belief despite evidence.

    Confirmation bias

    Seeking information that supports a belief.
    Confirmation bias is often how belief perseverance is maintained.

    Just-world phenomenon

    People get what they deserve.

    Fundamental attribution error (4.1)

    Blaming disposition, ignoring the situation.
    Both can lead to blaming victims; just-world adds the belief that life is fair.

    What you need to be able to do

    Would you give an obviously wrong answer just because everyone else did? Would you follow an order you thought was harmful? Would you help a stranger in trouble if a crowd was watching? Classic research says the situation matters far more than most of us like to admit.

    By the end of this page, you should be able to:

    • Explain social norms, normative and informational influence, and the conditions that increase conformity and obedience.
    • Explain persuasion: the central and peripheral routes, the halo effect, and the foot-in-the-door and door-in-the-face techniques.
    • Explain how culture and groups affect behavior: individualism and collectivism, group polarization, groupthink, diffusion of responsibility, social loafing, deindividuation, social facilitation and the false consensus effect.
    • Explain superordinate goals, social traps, I/O psychology and burnout, altruism and the bystander effect.
    How this shows up on the exam

    This topic is packed with terms that sound alike, so expect "which concept" questions. Group effects have different triggers: social loafing needs a group effort where individual work is hidden; deindividuation needs anonymity; social facilitation needs an audience. For conformity and obedience, know the conditions that make them stronger.

    Part 1

    Social influence, conformity and obedience

    Social norms are the expectations and roles a society has for its members: how to behave in a classroom, at a wedding, in a queue. Social influence theory says pressure to think or act a certain way comes in two forms:

    Normative influence

    Going along with others to be liked and accepted, or to avoid rejection.

    Laughing at a joke you don't find funny because everyone else is laughing.

    Informational influence

    Going along with others because you believe they know better.

    In a new country, copying what locals do at a ceremony because you assume they know what is correct.

    Conformity and obedience

    Conformity

    Changing your behavior or beliefs to match a group's unspoken rules or expectations. In Solomon Asch's studies, many people gave obviously wrong answers about line lengths when everyone else in the room did.

    More likely when: the group is unanimous, the person feels insecure, the group is attractive or high-status, and responses are public.

    Obedience

    Complying with the directives of an authority figure. In Stanley Milgram's studies, most participants kept giving what they believed were painful shocks to another person when an experimenter told them to.

    More likely when: the authority is close by and seen as legitimate, the victim is distant, and no one else refuses.

    Ethics link

    Milgram's studies are often used to discuss research ethics: participants experienced serious stress and were deceived. Expect a question on which ethical guideline was a concern.

    Part 2

    Persuasion

    Persuasion is the set of techniques used to convince yourself or others of an idea, action or belief. The elaboration likelihood model describes two routes:

    Central route

    Persuading with evidence and logical arguments. Works when people are motivated and able to think carefully, and tends to produce lasting change.

    A phone review comparing battery life, camera tests and price.

    Peripheral route

    Persuading with surface cues: attractiveness, celebrity, catchy music, emotion. Works when people are not thinking carefully; the change is often short-lived.

    A famous athlete smiling while holding the phone.

    The halo effect is a peripheral-route example: one positive trait (such as attractiveness) makes us assume a person has other positive traits too.

    Foot-in-the-door

    Start with a small request; once someone agrees, a bigger one is more likely to be accepted.

    "Can I borrow a pen?" then "Can I borrow your notes for the week?"

    Door-in-the-face

    Start with a large request that will be refused, then make the smaller request you actually wanted.

    "Can you lend me 500 dirhams?" "No." "How about 50?"

    Part 3

    Culture and groups

    Culture shapes how we see ourselves and others. Individualist cultures emphasize personal goals and independence; collectivist cultures emphasize group goals, family and interdependence. Multiculturalism, common in places like the UAE where many cultures live side by side, can broaden how people see themselves and others.

    Superordinate goals

    Shared goals that require groups to cooperate. They reduce hostility and stereotyping between groups.

    Rival year groups working together to raise money for a charity.

    Social traps

    Situations where individuals act in their own self-interest, and everyone ends up worse off.

    Everyone uses the air conditioning at full power, and the building's system fails.

    Industrial-organizational (I/O) psychologists study how people perform at work: the best ways to manage work, how coworkers relate to one another, and how people feel about their jobs, including burnout (exhaustion and loss of motivation from long-term work stress).

    Part 4

    Prosocial behavior

    Altruism

    Selfless concern for others and helping without expecting anything in return. Some researchers argue that people help partly because of social debt: a sense of obligation.

    Helping a stranger carry their bags with no reward.

    Social reciprocity norm

    The expectation that we should help those who have helped us.

    Helping a classmate revise because she helped you last month.

    Social responsibility norm

    The expectation that we should help those who depend on us or need help.

    Giving up your seat on the metro for an older passenger.

    Bystander effect

    People are less likely to help when others are present. Situational and attentional factors (how many people are there, whether they notice and interpret it as an emergency) predict helping.

    A person collapses in a busy mall, and everyone assumes someone else will act.

    Beat the bystander effect

    If you are the one who needs help in a crowd, point to one specific person: "You, in the blue shirt, call for help." That removes the diffusion of responsibility.

    Part 5

    Common mix-ups

    Normative influence

    Conform to be accepted.

    Informational influence

    Conform because others seem to know better.
    Ask: is the person worried about fitting in, or unsure of the right answer?

    Conformity

    Going along with the group.

    Obedience

    Following an authority's direct order.
    Peers suggest; authorities command.

    Foot-in-the-door

    Small request first, then bigger.

    Door-in-the-face

    Huge request first, then the real, smaller one.
    Foot = small step in. Door slammed in your face = big refusal first.

    Groupthink

    Harmony over good decisions.

    Group polarization

    Views become more extreme.
    Groupthink is about suppressing doubts; polarization is about views intensifying.

    Social loafing

    Less effort in a group.

    Social facilitation

    Better on easy tasks with an audience.
    Hidden in a group: loafing. Watched by others: facilitation.

    What you need to be able to do

    Why do you snap at your younger sibling after a bad day at school? Why do you insist you don't care about a grade you secretly care about a lot? This topic looks at two classic views of personality: one focused on hidden conflicts, and one focused on the drive to grow.

    By the end of this page, you should be able to:

    • Explain the psychodynamic view that unconscious processes drive personality.
    • Explain the eight ego defense mechanisms in the CED, with examples.
    • Explain how psychodynamic psychologists assess personality using projective tests.
    • Explain the humanistic view: unconditional positive regard and the self-actualizing tendency.

    Expect a short scenario and a question asking which defense mechanism it shows; write your own example for each of the eight so you can recognize them in new situations. For humanistic theory, know unconditional positive regard and the self-actualizing tendency.

    Not on the exam

    Freud's stage theory of development, and Maslow's hierarchy of needs.

    Part 1

    The psychodynamic view

    The psychodynamic theory, which grew out of Sigmund Freud's work, argues that unconscious processes drive personality: thoughts, wishes and memories we are not aware of still shape what we do. Freud described the mind as balancing three parts: the id (basic urges, wanting satisfaction now), the superego (our internalized sense of right and wrong) and the ego (the realistic part that tries to balance the two).

    Ego defense mechanisms

    When anxiety threatens the ego, it protects itself unconsciously with defense mechanisms. Test yourself: hide the names and identify each one from the example.

    Tap a blurred cell to reveal it.
    Defense mechanismWhat it doesExample
    DenialRefusing to accept a painful reality.Insisting a failing grade must be a mistake, and not checking the work.
    DisplacementRedirecting feelings toward a safer target.Being scolded by a teacher, then snapping at your younger brother at home.
    ProjectionSeeing your own unacceptable feelings in others.Someone who is jealous of a friend accuses the friend of being jealous of them.
    RationalizationMaking up a logical-sounding excuse for behavior."I didn't get into the team because the coach only picks his favorites."
    Reaction formationActing the opposite of how you really feel.Being overly friendly to a classmate you actually dislike.
    RegressionReturning to childlike behavior under stress.A teenager sulking and slamming doors like a young child after an argument.

    SublimationChanneling unacceptable impulses into socially acceptable activities.Turning anger into intense training at the gym.

    Assessing personality: projective tests

    Psychodynamic psychologists use projective tests, which present ambiguous material and ask people to interpret it, on the idea that their answers "project" preconscious and unconscious thoughts. Examples include inkblot tests and tests where people tell a story about an ambiguous picture. Critics point out that their scoring can be subjective, with weak reliability and validity (Topic 2.8).

    Part 2

    The humanistic view

    Humanistic psychologists, such as Carl Rogers, focused on healthy growth and saw people as basically good, with a natural drive to fulfill their potential. Two ideas are central:

    Unconditional positive regard

    Accepting and valuing a person completely, without conditions, even when you disapprove of what they did.

    A parent who makes clear that their love doesn't depend on grades.

    Self-actualizing tendency

    The natural drive to grow and become the best version of yourself, which humanists saw as a primary motivation.

    Taking up a challenge simply because it helps you grow.

    Rogers argued that people raised with conditional regard ("I'll love you if you succeed") may learn to hide parts of themselves, while unconditional positive regard helps them grow.

    Part 3

    Common mix-ups

    Displacement

    Feelings shifted to a safer target.

    Projection

    Your feelings seen in someone else.
    Displacement moves the target; projection moves the feeling onto another person.

    Repression

    Painful memories pushed out of awareness.

    Denial

    Refusing to accept a reality that is happening now.
    Repression hides the memory; denial rejects the fact.

    Reaction formation

    Doing the opposite of what you feel.

    Sublimation

    Channeling an impulse into something acceptable.
    Reaction formation reverses the feeling; sublimation redirects its energy usefully.

    Rationalization

    A believable excuse.

    Regression

    Childlike behavior.
    One sounds reasonable; the other looks young.

    Practice 4.4 questions
    Start here

    What you need to be able to do

    Are you the same person in class, at home and with your friends? Partly, yes, and partly, no. Social-cognitive theory says personality is shaped by a constant back-and-forth between you, your behavior and your surroundings. Trait theory looks for the stable patterns underneath, and tries to measure them.

    By the end of this page, you should be able to:

    • Explain reciprocal determinism and the social-cognitive view of personality.
    • Tell self-concept, self-efficacy and self-esteem apart.
    • Explain trait theories and the Big Five traits.
    • Explain how personality inventories and factor analysis are used to measure traits.
    How this shows up on the exam

    Self-efficacy and self-esteem are often confused: self-efficacy is about what you believe you can do; self-esteem is about how much you value yourself. For the Big Five, expect a description of behavior and a question asking which trait is high or low.

    Part 1

    The social-cognitive view

    The social-cognitive view, associated with Albert Bandura (you met his observational learning work in Topic 3.9), says personality is shaped by how we think about and interact with the world around us.

    Reciprocal determinism is the idea that three things constantly influence each other: our personal factors (thoughts, beliefs, traits), our behavior and our environment.

    Follow the arrows

    Hessa loves art (personal factor), so she joins the school art club (behavior). The club surrounds her with other artists and a teacher who encourages her (environment), which makes her even more confident and interested in art (personal factor), so she enters a competition (behavior)...

    How we see ourselves

    Part 2

    Trait theories and the Big Five

    Trait theories describe personality as a set of relatively stable characteristics that show up across situations. Instead of explaining why you are a certain way, they try to describe and measure how you are.

    The Big Five

    The best-supported trait model is the Big Five. Each trait is a spectrum: you can be anywhere from low to high.

    Tap a blurred cell to reveal it.
    TraitHigh scorers tend to beLow scorers tend to be
    Openness to experienceCurious, creative, keen on new ideas and experiences.Practical, prefer routine and the familiar.
    ConscientiousnessOrganized, reliable, careful, self-disciplined.Spontaneous, disorganized, careless with deadlines.
    ExtraversionOutgoing, talkative, energized by being with others.Reserved, quieter, energized by time alone.

    OCEAN

    Remember the Big Five as OCEAN: Openness, Conscientiousness, Extraversion, Agreeableness, and N for Neuroticism. Neuroticism is simply the other end of emotional stability: high neuroticism means low emotional stability.

    Measuring traits

    Personality inventories

    Questionnaires where people rate how well statements describe them ("I am the life of the party"). They are scored objectively, which gives them better reliability than projective tests (4.4).

    Rating "I get my homework done on time" from 1 (not like me) to 5 (very like me).

    Factor analysis

    A statistical method that finds which test items cluster together, revealing the underlying traits. This is how researchers arrived at five broad factors.

    Answers about being talkative, sociable and outgoing all correlate, so they form one factor: extraversion.

    Part 3

    Common mix-ups

    Self-efficacy

    "I can do this task."

    Self-esteem

    "I am worth something."
    Efficacy is about ability at a task; esteem is about overall worth.

    Self-concept

    Who you think you are.

    Self-esteem

    How you feel about who you are.
    Concept describes; esteem evaluates.

    Conscientiousness

    Organized and dependable.

    Agreeableness

    Kind and cooperative.
    A reliable but blunt person: high conscientiousness, low agreeableness.

    Personality inventory

    Objective questionnaire.

    Projective test (4.4)

    Interpreting ambiguous material.
    Inventories ask direct questions; projective tests look for hidden meaning.

    What you need to be able to do

    Why are you reading this right now? Maybe you're curious. Maybe there's a test next week. Maybe it's simply better than the alternative. Motivation is the set of forces that start, direct and keep up our behavior, and psychologists have several theories about where those forces come from.

    By the end of this page, you should be able to:

    • Explain drive-reduction theory, arousal theory and the Yerkes-Dodson law.
    • Explain self-determination theory (intrinsic and extrinsic motivation), incentive theory and instincts.
    • Explain Lewin's motivational conflicts: approach-approach, approach-avoidance and avoidance-avoidance.
    • Explain sensation seeking.
    • Explain the biological and external factors in eating, including ghrelin, leptin and the hypothalamus.
    How this shows up on the exam

    Expect a scenario asking which theory best explains a behavior: drinking water when thirsty (drive reduction), riding a roller coaster (arousal), working for a bonus (incentive or extrinsic). The Yerkes-Dodson law often appears with a graph, and Lewin's conflicts with a short dilemma.

    Part 1

    Theories of motivation

    Tap a blurred cell to reveal it.
    TheoryBig ideaExample
    Drive-reduction theoryBiological needs create a drive (an unpleasant state of tension). We are motivated to reduce it and return to homeostasis, the body's balanced, steady state.You're thirsty after football practice, so you drink water.
    Arousal theoryWe are motivated to keep an optimal level of arousal: if we are under-stimulated we seek excitement, and if over-stimulated we seek calm.Bored at home, you go for a drive; after a hectic week, you want a quiet weekend.
    Self-determination theoryWe are motivated by needs for competence, connection and control. It distinguishes intrinsic motivation (doing something for its own sake) from extrinsic motivation (doing it for outside rewards or to avoid punishment).Drawing because you love it (intrinsic) versus drawing to win a prize (extrinsic).
    Incentive theoryWe are pulled toward behaviors by rewards (incentives) and pushed away by punishments.Working extra shifts to earn a bonus.
    Instinct theoryInstincts are complex, unlearned behaviors shown by all members of a species. They are clear in non-human animals, but humans do not seem to show true instincts.Birds building nests or salmon swimming upstream to spawn.
    Push or pull?

    Drive reduction is a push from inside (a need). Incentive theory is a pull from outside (a reward).

    The Yerkes-Dodson law

    Linked to arousal theory, the Yerkes-Dodson law says performance is best at a moderate level of arousal. Too little and you're sluggish; too much and you panic. The best level depends on the task: easy or well-practiced tasks are performed best at higher arousal, while difficult or new tasks are performed best at lower arousal.

    Overjustification effect

    Paying people for something they already enjoy can reduce their intrinsic motivation. A child who loves reading may read less once you start paying them per book, because reading becomes "something I do for money".

    Part 2

    Conflicts and sensation seeking

    Kurt Lewin described three kinds of motivational conflict: situations in which we have to choose between goals.

    Tap a blurred cell to reveal it.
    ConflictWhat it isExample
    Approach-approachChoosing between two attractive options.Two universities you love have both offered you a place.
    Approach-avoidanceOne option with both attractive and unattractive features.A job abroad pays well but means leaving your family.
    Avoidance-avoidanceChoosing between two unattractive options.Staying up late to finish the essay or handing it in late.

    Sensation seeking

    Sensation seeking is the tendency to look for varied, new and intense experiences, and it differs from person to person. It has four parts:

    Experience seeking

    Looking for new sensations through the mind and senses.

    Traveling to unusual places, trying unfamiliar food.

    Thrill and adventure seeking

    Seeking physically exciting, risky activities.

    Skydiving, dune bashing, rock climbing.

    Disinhibition

    Seeking release through social activities and letting go of usual restraint.

    Being the loudest one at the party.

    Boredom susceptibility

    Strong dislike of repetition and routine.

    Getting restless when every day looks the same.

    Part 3

    Eating and belongingness

    Hunger is a good example of how biology, environment and social life work together to motivate behavior.

    Ghrelin

    A hormone released by the empty stomach that signals hunger.

    Ghrelin "growls": it makes you want to eat.

    Leptin

    A hormone released by fat cells that signals fullness and reduces appetite.

    Leptin helps you "leave" the table.

    These signals are monitored by the hypothalamus, which helps regulate eating through its control of the pituitary gland and the hormones it triggers (Topic 1.3).

    External factors matter too: the presence of food (you eat more when a plate of dates is in front of you), the time of day (feeling hungry at lunchtime because it is lunchtime) and social gatherings (eating more at a family iftar or a wedding).

    Belongingness

    Humans also have a strong need to belong: to form and keep close relationships. Being included motivates a lot of behavior, from joining teams to keeping up with friends online, and being rejected or left out is painful and can lower motivation and well-being.

    Part 4

    Common mix-ups

    Drive-reduction theory

    Reduce a biological need.

    Arousal theory

    Keep an optimal level of stimulation.
    Drive reduction can't explain why we seek excitement when we have no needs; arousal theory can.

    Intrinsic motivation

    For its own sake.

    Extrinsic motivation

    For rewards or to avoid punishment.
    Ask: would they still do it with no reward?

    Approach-avoidance

    One option, good and bad parts.

    Avoidance-avoidance

    Two bad options.
    Count the options first, then judge if they're attractive.

    Ghrelin

    Hunger signal from the stomach.

    Leptin

    Fullness signal from fat cells.
    Ghrelin grows hunger; leptin lessens it.

    What you need to be able to do

    Your heart pounds before your name is called for a speech. Is that fear, or excitement? Is the pounding heart the cause of the feeling, the result of it, or something you have to interpret? This topic is about what emotions are, how early theories tried to explain them, and how culture shapes how we show them.

    By the end of this page, you should be able to:

    • Explain what emotion is.
    • Explain early theories of emotion as different sequences of body response, thought and feeling.
    • Explain the facial-feedback hypothesis and the broaden-and-build theory.
    • Explain the research on universal emotions, and how display rules and emotion triggers differ across cultures.
    How this shows up on the exam

    You will not be asked to name the early theories, but you should be able to describe their different sequences and apply them to a scenario. Know that research on the facial-feedback hypothesis and on universal emotions has mixed results.

    Not on the exam

    The specific names of the early theories of emotion. They are included below as optional background only.

    Part 1

    What emotion is and early theories

    An emotion (or affect) is a subjective experience that involves physical arousal, expressive behavior and conscious thought, and it can shape how we think and act.

    Early theories disagreed about the order in which these parts happen. Picture yourself waiting backstage before a speech:

    1. The body comes first
    Event→Body responds→Emotion felt
    2. Body and feeling at the same time
    Event→Body respondsEmotion felt
    3. The body needs a label
    Event→Body responds→Cognitive label→Emotion felt
    Tap a blurred cell to reveal it.
    SequenceWhat it claimsBackstage example
    The body comes firstThe physical response happens first, and we feel the emotion when we notice it."My heart is pounding, so I must be afraid."
    At the same timeThe physical response and the emotion happen together, independently.Your heart pounds and you feel afraid at the same moment.
    The body needs a labelArousal on its own is not enough; we need to interpret it with a cognitive label based on the situation."My heart is pounding, and I'm about to give a speech, so this is nervousness." (At a football final, the same arousal might be labeled excitement.)
    Optional background

    These sequences match three classic theories: the body first (James-Lange), at the same time (Cannon-Bard) and the cognitive label (Schachter-Singer two-factor theory). You don't need the names for the exam, but you may see them in textbooks.

    Part 2

    Expressing emotion

    Facial-feedback hypothesis

    Our facial expressions can influence the emotions we feel, not just reveal them. Research results on this have been mixed.

    Holding a pen between your teeth (forcing a smile) may make cartoons seem slightly funnier.

    Broaden-and-build theory

    Positive emotions broaden our thinking and attention, which helps us build skills, relationships and resources over time.

    Feeling happy and relaxed, you try a new activity with friends and pick up a new skill.

    Emotion across cultures

    Paul Ekman's research suggested that six basic emotions (happiness, sadness, anger, fear, surprise and disgust) are expressed and recognized with similar facial expressions across cultures. However, findings on how universal these expressions are have been mixed, and culture clearly shapes emotion in two ways:

    Display rules

    Cultural norms about when, where and how strongly it is appropriate to show emotions.

    In some cultures, showing strong anger to an elder in public is seen as deeply disrespectful, so people hide it.

    Elicitors

    What triggers an emotion can differ across cultures and other groups, such as age and social class.

    A hand gesture that is friendly in one country may be offensive, and cause anger, in another.

    Living it

    In a multicultural school like ours, you have probably seen display rules in action: some classmates celebrate loudly and openly, while others show the same happiness with a quiet smile. Same emotion, different rules.

    Part 3

    Common mix-ups

    The body comes first

    Arousal, then emotion.

    The body needs a label

    Arousal plus interpretation, then emotion.
    If the scenario mentions the person reading the situation to explain their arousal, it's the label sequence.

    Facial-feedback hypothesis

    Expressions shape emotions.

    Display rules

    Culture shapes how we show emotions.
    One works from face to feeling; the other from culture to face.

    Display rules

    How and when emotions are shown.

    Elicitors

    What triggers the emotion.
    Rules govern the showing; elicitors govern the triggering.
