    What you need to be able to do

    Look at a cloud long enough and you may see a face in it. The cloud has not changed: your brain has. Sensation (Topic 1.6) collects the raw information. Perception is what your brain does with it: organizing it, filling gaps and deciding what it means, using both what is out there and what you already expect.

    By the end of this page, you should be able to:

    • Explain bottom-up and top-down processing, and how schemas, perceptual sets, context, experience and culture shape perception.
    • Identify the four Gestalt principles in the CED: closure, figure and ground, proximity and similarity.
    • Explain selective attention, the cocktail party effect, and blindness caused by inattention, including change blindness.
    • Identify binocular and monocular depth cues, perceptual constancy and apparent movement.
    How this shows up on the exam

    Expect a short scenario (someone reading messy handwriting, a driver judging distance, a person who misses something obvious) and a question asking which perceptual process or cue explains it. Be precise: the exam distinguishes, for example, relative size from linear perspective.

    Not on the exam

    Any monocular depth cue beyond the five on this page.

    Part 1

    Bottom-up and top-down processing

    Bottom-up processing starts with the raw sensory information and builds up to a perception, piece by piece. Top-down processing starts with what you already know and expect, and uses that to interpret the information. Most of the time you use both at once.

    Try it: is it a letter or a number?

    Read across, then read down. The middle symbol is exactly the same in both directions.

    A 121314 C

    Read across, it looks like the number 13. Read down, between A and C, it looks like the letter B. The marks on the screen did not change; your expectation did. That is top-down processing.

    Part 2

    Gestalt principles: organizing what you see

    Gestalt psychologists argued that we perceive whole patterns, not separate pieces: the whole is different from the sum of its parts. The CED names four principles that explain how we organize what we see.

    Closure

    We fill in gaps to see a complete, whole object. You see a circle, not eight curved lines.

    Figure and ground

    We separate an object (the figure) from its background (the ground). Is this a white circle on a dark square, or a dark frame with a hole?

    Proximity

    We group things that are close together. You see three groups of four dots, not twelve separate dots.

    Similarity

    We group things that look alike. The evenly spaced dots read as rows, because each row shares a color.

    Memory hook

    Closure closes gaps. Figure and ground: what stands out and what sits behind. Proximity = nearby belongs together. Similarity = same-looking belongs together.

    Part 3

    Attention

    Attention is where sensation and perception meet. Far more information reaches your senses than you could ever process, so attention selects what gets through, shaped by both internal factors (your goals, your interests) and external ones (a sudden noise, a bright color).

    Selective attention and the cocktail party effect

    Focusing on one thing while filtering out the rest. In a loud school cafeteria you can follow one friend's conversation, yet you still notice instantly when someone across the room says your name. That is the cocktail party effect.

    Filtered out, but not completely ignored.

    Blindness from inattention

    When attention is elsewhere, we can miss things that are right in front of us. In a well-known study, many viewers counting basketball passes completely missed a person in a gorilla suit walking through the game. Change blindness is a specific form: failing to notice that something in the scene has changed.

    Looking is not the same as seeing.

    Spot the difference

    Missing something new that appears while you are focused elsewhere is blindness from inattention. Failing to notice that something already there has changed (a different person, a moved object, a missing detail between two film shots) is change blindness.

    Part 4

    Depth perception

    Your retina is a flat surface, yet you see a three-dimensional world. Your brain rebuilds depth from cues.

    Binocular cues: need both eyes

    Retinal disparity

    Each eye sees a slightly different image, and the brain compares them. The bigger the difference, the closer the object.

    Hold up a finger and close each eye in turn: it jumps.

    Convergence

    Your eyes turn inward to focus on something close. The more they turn in, the closer the brain judges the object to be.

    Bring a finger toward your nose and feel your eyes cross.

    Monocular cues: work with one eye (and in photos)

    Monocular cues give an impression of depth even on flat, two-dimensional surfaces like a photo or a painting.

    Texture gradientTextures look coarse and detailed up close and become finer and denser with distance.Ripples in the sand are clear at your feet and blur into smooth sand toward the horizon.

    InterpositionAn object that blocks part of another is seen as closer.A car partly hiding a building is seen as in front of it.
    Part 5

    Constancy and apparent movement

    Perceptual constancy

    We keep perceiving an object as the same even when its image on the retina changes. A friend walking away gets smaller on your retina, but you do not think she is shrinking (size constancy). A door swinging open changes shape on your retina, but you still see a rectangle (shape constancy). A white shirt looks white in shade and in sunlight (brightness and color constancy).

    Stable world, changing images.

    Apparent movement

    We can perceive movement where none exists. A film or a phone animation is a rapid series of still images, and a strip of lights switching on and off in sequence looks like a single light moving along.

    Still images, moving perception.

    Part 6

    Common mix-ups

    Bottom-up processing

    Starts from the raw sensory details.

    Top-down processing

    Starts from knowledge and expectations.
    If the scenario mentions expectations, prior knowledge or context, it is top-down.

    Schema

    A general mental framework for a category.

    Perceptual set

    A temporary readiness to see something specific.
    A perceptual set is often created by what someone was just told or expects to see.

    Binocular cues

    Need both eyes: retinal disparity and convergence.

    Monocular cues

    Work with one eye and in flat images.
    If the cue works in a photograph, it must be monocular.

    Relative size

    Compares the size of two similar objects.

    Linear perspective

    Uses parallel lines that seem to converge.
    Two similar objects? Relative size. Lines meeting in the distance? Linear perspective.

    Inattention blindness

    Missing something new that appears.

    Change blindness

    Missing that something has changed.
    Both happen because attention was elsewhere.

    What you need to be able to do

    Every day you make hundreds of decisions, most of them in less than a second. Which queue is shorter? Is this news story true? Should you keep watching a series you are not enjoying because you are already five episodes in? This topic is about the shortcuts and traps in how we think, solve problems and decide.

    By the end of this page, you should be able to:

    • Explain concepts, prototypes and schemas, including assimilation and accommodation.
    • Compare algorithms and heuristics, and explain the representativeness and availability heuristics.
    • Explain how mental set, priming, framing, the gambler's fallacy and the sunk-cost fallacy affect decisions.
    • Explain executive functions, creativity, divergent and convergent thinking, and functional fixedness.
    How this shows up on the exam

    Most questions describe a person's reasoning and ask which heuristic, bias or fallacy it shows. The classic mix-up is availability versus representativeness: availability is about how easily examples come to mind; representativeness is about how well something matches a prototype or stereotype.

    Part 1

    Concepts, prototypes and schemas

    Concepts are the mental categories that form the building blocks of thought: "bird", "furniture", "teacher". For each concept we hold a prototype, the best or most typical example. Prototypes can vary with experience: for many people in the UAE, a falcon may come to mind for "bird" faster than it would for someone elsewhere.

    Schemas are broader frameworks for thinking about how something works. We keep them up to date in two ways:

    Assimilation

    Taking in new information and fitting it into an existing schema without changing the schema.

    A toddler who knows horses sees a camel and calls it a horse.

    Accommodation

    Changing the schema to fit new information that does not match.

    The toddler learns that camels are a separate kind of animal, and creates a new category.

    Part 2

    Solving problems: algorithms and heuristics

    Algorithm

    A step-by-step method that tries every possible solution until the right one is found. It guarantees an answer, but can be very slow.

    Trying every combination of a three-digit lock, from 000 to 999.

    Heuristic

    A mental shortcut or rule of thumb. Fast and usually good enough, but it can lead to errors in judgment.

    Guessing the lock code is the owner's birthday.

    Two heuristics that can mislead you

    Representativeness heuristic

    Judging how likely something is by how well it matches your prototype or stereotype, while ignoring how common it actually is.

    Assuming a quiet student who loves poetry must study literature, not engineering, even though far more students study engineering.

    Availability heuristic

    Judging how likely something is by how easily examples come to mind, especially recent or vivid ones.

    After seeing plane crash footage all week, feeling flying is more dangerous than driving, though driving is far riskier.

    Part 4

    Executive functions and creativity

    Executive functions are the cognitive processes that let you generate, organize, plan and carry out goal-directed behavior and think critically: making a revision timetable, sticking to it, and adjusting when something comes up. (They depend heavily on the prefrontal cortex, Topic 1.4.)

    Creativity is the ability to produce novel and useful ideas.

    Divergent thinking

    Generating many different possible answers. Creativity depends on it.

    List as many uses for a paperclip as you can in one minute.

    Convergent thinking

    Narrowing down to the single best answer.

    A multiple-choice question with one correct option.

    What blocks creative thinking

    Functional fixedness is the tendency to see objects only in terms of their usual function. Needing a screwdriver and not realizing a coin could turn the screw is functional fixedness. It is the opposite of divergent thinking.

    Part 5

    Common mix-ups

    Availability heuristic

    How easily examples come to mind.

    Representativeness heuristic

    How well something matches a prototype.
    Ask: is the person recalling vivid examples, or comparing to a stereotype?

    Assimilation

    New information fits the old schema; the schema stays the same.

    Accommodation

    The schema changes to fit new information.
    Accommodation is the one where the person changes their mind.

    Mental set

    Repeating a strategy that worked before.

    Functional fixedness

    Seeing an object only in its usual role.
    Mental set is about methods; functional fixedness is about objects.

    Gambler's fallacy

    Thinks past chance events change future odds.

    Sunk-cost fallacy

    Keeps going because of what has already been spent.
    One is about chance; the other is about investment.

    Priming

    Earlier exposure nudges later responses.

    Framing

    The wording of the choice changes the decision.
    Priming happens before; framing is in how the question itself is presented.

    What you need to be able to do

    You remember your first day at this school, that Abu Dhabi is the UAE's capital, how to ride a bike, and (hopefully) that your homework is due on Thursday. Those are four very different kinds of memory. This topic sorts them out and introduces the three models psychologists use to explain how memory works.

    By the end of this page, you should be able to:

    • Tell apart explicit (episodic and semantic), implicit (procedural) and prospective memory.
    • Explain long-term potentiation as a biological basis of memory.
    • Describe the multi-store model, including sensory memory, automatic and effortful processing.
    • Describe the working memory model and its three components.
    • Explain the levels of processing model: structural, phonemic and semantic.
    How this shows up on the exam

    Expect to classify a memory from a description ("remembering your cousin's wedding" is episodic) and to identify which model or component a scenario describes. Students often treat the multi-store model and the working memory model as the same thing: they are not.

    Part 1

    Types of memory

    Tap a box to see what that kind of memory is.

    Memory

    Memories for knowledge, events and experiences differ in how they are processed, stored and retrieved. The main split is between memories you can easily describe (explicit) and those you cannot (implicit).

    Explicit memory

    Memory you can consciously recall and easily describe or explain to someone else. Sometimes called declarative memory. It comes in two types: episodic and semantic.

    Episodic memory

    Memory for personal experiences and events, tied to a time and place: your first day at this school, what happened at your cousin's wedding.

    Hook: Episodic = episodes of your life.

    Semantic memory

    Memory for facts and general knowledge, not tied to when you learned them: Abu Dhabi is the capital of the UAE; the hippocampus forms new memories.

    Hook: Semantic = sense and meaning, facts.

    Implicit memory

    Memory that shows up in what you do, but is hard to describe or explain in words. You know it without knowing how you know it.

    Procedural memory

    A type of implicit memory for skills and procedures: riding a bike, typing on your phone, tying a headscarf or shoelaces. Try explaining exactly how you keep your balance on a bike: it is surprisingly hard.

    Hook: Procedural = how to do it.

    Prospective memory

    Memory for future actions: remembering to submit your homework on Thursday, or to call your grandmother after school. It is remembering to remember.

    The biology: long-term potentiation

    Long-term potentiation is the strengthening of synaptic connections between neurons when they are activated together frequently. Each time you practice retrieving a fact, the connection behind it becomes a little easier to activate. It is the biological process thought to underlie memory, and it links back to neural firing in Topic 1.3.

    Memory hook

    Neurons that fire together, wire together.

    Part 2

    Three models of memory

    1. The multi-store model

    Information must pass through three interacting systems to be remembered. Most of what hits your senses never makes it past the first one.

    • Sensory memory holds a brief, detailed copy of what your senses take in: iconic memory for sights (a fraction of a second) and echoic memory for sounds (a few seconds; why you can "replay" what someone just said when you weren't listening).
    • What you pay attention to moves into short-term memory, which holds a small amount for a short time.
    • Information that is encoded moves into long-term memory, which is long-lasting and has huge capacity. Retrieval brings it back when you need it.

    The model highlights two kinds of processing. Automatic processing happens without effort or awareness (you remember roughly where you sat at lunch without trying). Effortful processing needs attention and work (memorizing the stages of sleep).

    2. The working memory model

    This model zooms in on what you are actively doing with information right now. Your working memory is an active workspace with three parts that work together to process information into long-term memory.

    • The central executive directs attention and coordinates the other parts, like a manager.
    • The phonological loop holds sounds and words: repeating a phone number in your head.
    • The visuospatial sketchpad holds images and spatial layouts: picturing the route from your classroom to the library.

    3. The levels of processing model

    How deeply you process information determines how well you remember it, from shallowest to deepest:

    Common mix-ups

    Episodic memory

    Personal events with a time and place.

    Semantic memory

    Facts and knowledge.
    "Remembering your graduation" is episodic; "knowing what graduation means" is semantic.

    Explicit memory

    Easy to describe in words.

    Implicit memory

    Shows in what you do; hard to put into words.
    Procedural memory is implicit.

    Short-term memory (multi-store)

    A temporary store that holds information briefly.

    Working memory

    An active workspace that manipulates information.
    If the scenario involves doing something with information (mental math, picturing a route), think working memory.

    Automatic processing

    Encoding without effort or awareness.

    Effortful processing

    Encoding that needs attention and work.
    Both come from the multi-store model.

    Phonemic processing

    Focus on sound.

    Semantic processing

    Focus on meaning.
    Semantic is the deepest level and gives the best recall.

    What you need to be able to do

    Encoding is getting information into memory in the first place. If it never goes in properly, no amount of last-minute panic will get it out. The good news: how you encode is largely up to you. This topic is the psychology behind the study strategies that actually work.

    By the end of this page, you should be able to:

    • Explain mnemonic devices, including the method of loci.
    • Explain how chunking, categories and hierarchies improve encoding.
    • Explain the spacing effect: massed versus distributed practice.
    • Explain the serial position effect, including the primacy and recency effects.
    How this shows up on the exam

    Expect a scenario describing how someone studies or what they remember from a list, and a question asking which encoding strategy or effect is at work. This topic also lends itself to data questions, such as reading a serial position graph.

    Part 1

    Mnemonics and organizing information

    How information is encoded determines how well it is stored and how easily it can be retrieved later. The CED names several ways to encode more effectively.

    Mnemonic devices

    Mnemonic devices are memory aids that help encode information into working and long-term memory: acronyms, rhymes, vivid images. The method of loci is a powerful one: you imagine walking through a familiar place, such as your home, and "place" each item you need to remember in a different spot. To recall them, you take the same walk in your mind.

    Organizing information

    Chunking

    Grouping individual items into meaningful units, so short-term memory holds a few chunks instead of many items.

    See the phone number below.

    Categories

    Sorting items into groups by type.

    A shopping list grouped into fruit, dairy and bread.

    Hierarchies

    Organizing information from broad categories down to specific details.

    The nervous system map in Topic 1.2: nervous system, then CNS and PNS, then their branches.

    Try it: chunking a phone number

    Glance at each version for two seconds, look away, and try to repeat it.

    971501234567
    +971  50  123  4567

    Same twelve digits. The second version is four meaningful chunks (country code, network, then two groups), which is far easier to hold in short-term memory.

    Part 2

    The spacing effect

    The spacing effect is the finding that you remember far more when study is spread out over time than when it is crammed into one session, even if the total time is the same.

    Massed practice (cramming)

    Studying all at once, such as six hours the night before a test. It can feel productive, and it may get you through tomorrow, but much of it fades quickly.

    Six hours, one night.

    Distributed practice

    Spreading study across several shorter sessions with gaps in between. Each session strengthens the memory and consolidation has time to work.

    One hour, six times, across two weeks.

    Use this, don't just learn it

    Distributed practice is one of the best-supported study strategies in all of psychology. For the AP exam in May, a little Unit 2 review every week will beat a frantic week of cramming in April.

    Part 3

    The serial position effect

    The order in which information is presented affects how well it is encoded. Read someone a list of fifteen words and ask them to recall as many as they can: they will remember the first few and the last few best, and lose the middle. This is the serial position effect.

    Primacy effect

    Items at the beginning are remembered well, because there was time to rehearse them and move them into long-term memory.

    The first names you hear at a party.

    Recency effect

    Items at the end are remembered well, because they are still in short-term memory.

    The last few things your teacher said before the bell.

    One extra detail worth knowing: if recall is delayed instead of immediate, the recency effect tends to fade (those items have left short-term memory), while the primacy effect remains.

    Part 4

    Common mix-ups

    Primacy effect

    Better recall for the first items.

    Recency effect

    Better recall for the last items.
    Primary school comes first; recent events happened last.

    Massed practice

    All at once; feels efficient, fades fast.

    Distributed practice

    Spread out over time; lasts longer.
    The spacing effect is the reason distributed practice wins.

    Chunking

    Grouping items into meaningful units.

    Method of loci

    Placing items along an imagined familiar route.
    Chunking organizes; the method of loci uses imagery and location.

    What you need to be able to do

    Some memories vanish in a second; others last a lifetime. This topic compares the different memory stores by how long they last and how much they hold, explains how rehearsal keeps information alive, and looks at what happens when storage itself is damaged.

    By the end of this page, you should be able to:

    • Compare sensory, short-term, working and long-term memory by duration, capacity and content.
    • Explain maintenance rehearsal and elaborative rehearsal.
    • Explain highly superior autobiographical memory and why self-related memories are easier to keep.
    • Explain retrograde and anterograde amnesia, Alzheimer's disease and infantile amnesia.
    How this shows up on the exam

    Expect to compare the memory stores, to pick the more effective type of rehearsal in a study scenario, and to tell retrograde from anterograde amnesia in a case description. That last pair is a favorite, so learn which direction each one points.

    Part 1

    How long, how much, what kind

    The memory systems from Topic 2.3 differ in three ways: how long they hold information (duration), how much they hold (capacity) and what kind of information they hold (content).

    Tap a blurred cell to reveal it.
    StoreDurationCapacityContent
    Sensory memoryA fraction of a second (sights) to a few seconds (sounds)Large: a brief copy of everything the senses take inRaw sensory information
    Short-term memoryAround 20 to 30 seconds without rehearsalSmall: roughly 7 items, give or take 2Whatever you are paying attention to right now
    Working memoryAs long as you keep actively using itLimitedInformation you are actively manipulating: solving, comparing, planning
    Long-term memoryMinutes to a lifetimeEssentially unlimitedKnowledge, skills and experiences

    Keeping information alive: rehearsal

    Maintenance rehearsal

    Repeating information over and over to keep it in memory. It prolongs storage, but adds little meaning.

    Repeating a Wi-Fi password until you have typed it in.

    Elaborative rehearsal

    Rehearsing in a way that connects the information to meaning: linking it to what you know, to examples, to your own life. It leads to much better long-term retention.

    Explaining "negative reinforcement" using a time it happened to you.

    Part 2

    Superior memory and the self

    A small number of people have highly superior autobiographical memory: give them almost any date from their adult life and they can tell you what day of the week it was and what they did. Cases like these suggest there may be biological processes that allow superior memory storage.

    For everyone else, autobiographical memory (memory for the events of your own life) helps explain a useful pattern: information connected to ourselves is more memorable. Linking a new term to your own experience is a form of elaborative rehearsal, and it works.

    Make it personal

    When you learn a new term, ask: "When has this happened to me?" Connecting it to your own life gives it a stronger place in memory.

    Part 3

    When storage breaks down

    Storage can be damaged by physical injury or illness, and it is limited by development.

    Retrograde amnesia

    Loss of memories from before the injury or illness. The person may not remember the weeks leading up to an accident.

    Retro = backward in time.

    Anterograde amnesia

    Inability to form new memories after the injury, while older memories may stay intact. The person may meet you every day and never remember you.

    Antero = forward in time.

    Alzheimer's disease

    A progressive brain disease that gradually destroys memory. It often begins with difficulty forming new memories and, over time, erodes older ones too.

    Infantile amnesia

    Most people have almost no memories from before about age 3 or 4. The brain systems needed to store lasting explicit memories are still developing.

    A developmental limit, not damage.

    Part 4

    Common mix-ups

    Retrograde amnesia

    Can't remember the past, before the injury.

    Anterograde amnesia

    Can't make new memories after the injury.
    Draw the timeline: retrograde points back, anterograde points forward.

    Maintenance rehearsal

    Repetition; keeps information briefly.

    Elaborative rehearsal

    Adds meaning; stores it better long term.
    If the student links it to examples or their own life, it is elaborative.

    Short-term memory

    A brief, small store.

    Long-term memory

    A lasting, essentially unlimited store.
    Short-term memory holds about 7 items; long-term memory has no known limit.

    What you need to be able to do

    You know you studied it. You can almost see the page. But in the exam hall, the answer will not come. Retrieval is getting information back out of memory, and this topic explains why it sometimes fails and how to make it far more reliable.

    By the end of this page, you should be able to:

    • Compare recall and recognition, and explain the role of retrieval cues.
    • Explain context-dependent, mood-congruent and state-dependent memory.
    • Explain the testing effect and metacognition as forms of retrieval practice.
    How this shows up on the exam

    Expect scenarios like "she remembers more when she studies and tests in the same room" and a question asking which retrieval effect it shows. Context (the place), mood (the feeling) and state (the physical condition) are easy to confuse, so match each to its clue word.

    Part 1

    Recall and recognition

    Recall

    Retrieving information without cues: you have to produce it yourself.

    A fill-in-the-blank question, or a free-response answer.

    Recognition

    Identifying information when it is presented to you, using retrieval cues.

    A multiple-choice question: the right answer is in front of you.

    Recognition is usually easier than recall, which is why you can recognize a classmate's face years later even if you could never recall their name unprompted.

    Part 2

    Matching the moment

    Retrieval improves when your situation at retrieval matches your situation when you encoded the information. The match acts as a retrieval cue.

    Tap a blurred cell to reveal it.
    EffectWhat has to matchExample
    Context-dependent memoryThe environment or placeYou walk into the kitchen and forget why, then remember once you are back at your desk.
    Mood-congruent memoryYour moodWhen you are feeling low, sad memories come to mind more easily; when happy, happy ones do.
    State-dependent memoryYour physical stateMaterial learned while very tired, or after strong coffee, is recalled better in that same physical state.
    Clue words

    Place or room: context. Feeling or emotion: mood. Body or physical condition: state.

    Part 3

    Retrieval practice

    Successful retrieval becomes more likely with retrieval practice: deliberately pulling information out of memory, rather than just putting it in again.

    The testing effect

    Remembering is enhanced by retrieving information, not just rereading it. Testing yourself is a way of learning, not only of checking.

    Answering practice questions beats highlighting your notes again.

    Metacognition

    Thinking about your own thinking: judging what you really know and what you only recognize, then adjusting how you study.

    "I can recognize these terms, but can I explain them without looking?"

    Use this before May

    Rereading feels comfortable, because the material looks familiar. That feeling is recognition, not recall. The practice quizzes on this site use the testing effect, so do them before you feel ready, not after.

    Part 4

    Common mix-ups

    Recall

    No cues: you produce the answer.

    Recognition

    Cues present: you identify the answer.
    Free-response is recall; multiple-choice is recognition.

    Context-dependent

    Same place.

    State-dependent

    Same physical condition.
    A room is context; being tired or caffeinated is state.

    Mood-congruent memory

    Your current mood brings up matching memories.

    State-dependent memory

    Your physical state matches the learning state.
    Mood is emotional; state is physical.

    What you need to be able to do

    Forgetting is not always failure. Your brain is constantly deciding what to keep, and it rebuilds memories every time you recall them. This topic explains why we forget, and why a memory can feel completely certain and still be wrong.

    By the end of this page, you should be able to:

    • Interpret the forgetting curve.
    • Explain encoding failure, proactive and retroactive interference, and inadequate retrieval (the tip-of-the-tongue phenomenon).
    • Explain the psychodynamic idea of repression.
    • Explain the misinformation effect, source amnesia and constructive memory, including imagination inflation.
    How this shows up on the exam

    Proactive versus retroactive interference is one of the most tested pairs in the whole course: be sure which direction each one runs. Expect scenarios about eyewitnesses and false memories for the misinformation effect and source amnesia, and graph questions on the forgetting curve.

    Part 1

    The forgetting curve

    The forgetting curve, first mapped by Hermann Ebbinghaus, shows that time is a major factor in forgetting. Most forgetting happens quickly, soon after learning, and then the curve levels off: what survives the first few days tends to last.

    The study lesson

    The steep early drop is why reviewing a lesson within a day or two (distributed practice, Topic 2.4) is so effective: it catches the memory before most of it slips away.

    Part 2

    Why we forget

    Tap a blurred cell to reveal it.
    ReasonWhat happensExample
    Encoding failureThe information never got into long-term memory in the first place.You have handled one-dirham coins a thousand times, but could you draw the front from memory?
    Proactive interferenceOld learning gets in the way of remembering new information.You keep typing your old password after changing it.
    Retroactive interferenceNew learning gets in the way of remembering old information.After learning your new class timetable, you cannot remember last year's.
    Inadequate retrievalThe memory is stored, but you cannot access it right now.The tip-of-the-tongue phenomenon: you know the actor's name, you know it starts with M, but it will not come.
    Memory hook for interference

    Proactive: the old memory moves forward and blocks the new. Retroactive: the new memory reaches back and blocks the old. Ask which memory is the one you are failing to remember: if it is the new one, it is proactive.

    Repression: a psychodynamic explanation

    Psychodynamic theorists, following Freud, believe some memories are forgotten to protect the ego from distress: the mind pushes painful memories out of conscious awareness. This is called repression. Many researchers question how often this really happens, but you need to know what the psychodynamic perspective claims.

    Part 3

    When memories are wrong

    Memory is not a video recording. Each time you recall something, you rebuild it, and the rebuilt version can change.

    Misinformation effect

    Misleading information introduced after an event distorts the memory of it. In classic studies, people who were asked how fast cars were going when they "smashed" into each other gave higher speed estimates, and were more likely to falsely remember broken glass, than people asked about cars that "hit" each other.

    The wording of the question rewrote the memory.

    Source amnesia

    Remembering information but forgetting where it came from. You are sure your teacher said a "fact" that you actually saw in a social media video.

    The content stays; the source fades.

    Constructive memory

    Memories are reconstructed each time they are recalled and consolidated again, so gaps get filled with what seems likely.

    Your memory of a family trip slowly absorbs details from the photos and stories.

    Imagination inflation

    Repeatedly imagining an event that never happened increases your confidence that it did.

    Picturing yourself getting lost as a child can make you more confident it really happened.

    Why this matters beyond the exam

    These findings are why psychologists urge caution about eyewitness memory and leading questions: a confident memory is not always an accurate one.

    Part 4

    Common mix-ups

    Proactive interference

    Old blocks new.

    Retroactive interference

    New blocks old.
    Old password typed by mistake: proactive. Forgetting last year's timetable: retroactive.

    Encoding failure

    It never went in.

    Inadequate retrieval

    It went in, but you can't get it out right now.
    Tip of the tongue is a retrieval failure, not an encoding failure.

    Misinformation effect

    Later information changes the memory itself.

    Source amnesia

    The memory is fine; its source is forgotten.
    Ask whether the content changed or only the source was lost.

    Repression

    Forgetting to protect the ego (a psychodynamic idea).

    Ordinary forgetting

    Time, interference, encoding or retrieval failure.
    If the memory is distressing and pushed out of awareness, the answer is repression.

    What you need to be able to do

    What does it mean to be "smart"? Is it one ability or many? Can a test measure it fairly? And what happens when test scores are used to decide people's futures? This topic covers how psychologists have tried to answer these questions, and where they got it wrong.

    By the end of this page, you should be able to:

    • Explain the debate over whether intelligence is a general ability (g) or several abilities.
    • Explain how IQ was calculated, and what makes a test standardized, valid and reliable.
    • Explain stereotype threat, stereotype lift and the Flynn effect.
    • Explain how intelligence scores can be affected by bias and inequity, and how they have been misused.
    • Compare achievement and aptitude tests, and fixed and growth mindsets.
    How this shows up on the exam

    Expect questions on psychometrics: which type of reliability or validity a scenario describes, or whether a test is reliable but not valid. You should also be ready to use the Flynn effect and within-group variation to evaluate claims about group differences in IQ.

    Not on the exam

    Labeling or describing particular cognitive abilities and disabilities.

    Part 1

    What is intelligence?

    Psychologists still do not agree on exactly how to define intelligence, and definitions and measures have been subject to bias throughout history. The central debate:

    One general ability (g)

    Intelligence is a single general ability, called g, that underlies performance on many different tasks. People who do well on one kind of test tend to do well on others.

    Associated with Charles Spearman.

    Multiple abilities

    Intelligence is made up of several different abilities that can be strong or weak independently, such as musical, interpersonal or practical intelligence.

    Associated with Howard Gardner and Robert Sternberg.

    (The CED does not require the theorists' names, but they often appear in questions and textbooks.)

    Part 2

    Measuring intelligence

    Try it: the original IQ formula

    Early tests compared a child's mental age (the age level they performed at) with their chronological age (their actual age).

    IQ = mental age ÷ chronological age × 100. Modern tests no longer use this formula; they compare your score with others your age, with 100 set as the average.

    Today, IQ scores are often used to identify students for educational services, such as extra support or advanced programs.

    What makes a test useful?

    Every psychological test, intelligence tests included, should follow sound psychometric principles.

    Tap a blurred cell to reveal it.
    PrincipleMeaningTypes you need to know
    StandardizedGiven and scored using the same procedures and conditions for everyone, so scores can be compared fairly.Same instructions, same time limit, same scoring rules
    ValidMeasures what it claims to measure.Construct validity: does it really measure the idea (the construct) it is meant to? Predictive validity: does it predict the future behavior it should, such as success at university?
    ReliableGives consistent results each time.Test-retest: similar scores when the same people take it again later. Split-half: the two halves of the test (say, odd and even questions) give similar scores.
    The bathroom scale test

    A scale that always shows you 5 kg heavier than you really are is reliable (same reading every time) but not valid (wrong reading). A test can be reliable without being valid, but it cannot be valid without being reliable.

    Part 3

    Fairness, bias and misuse

    Stereotype threat

    Worry about confirming a negative stereotype about your group can lower your performance. For example, older adults who are reminded beforehand that memory declines with age tend to score lower on a memory test.

    The worry itself uses up attention.

    Stereotype lift

    A performance boost that can come from being compared with a group that is negatively stereotyped. It can widen gaps in scores that have nothing to do with ability.

    The flip side of threat.

    For these reasons, researchers try to develop intelligence tests that are socio-culturally responsive, reducing stereotype threat and the unfair gaps that stereotype lift can create.

    Systemic issues in intelligence testing

    • The Flynn effect: average IQ scores across much of the world have risen over the past century. Genes cannot change that fast, so the rise points to societal factors such as better nutrition, better health care and higher living standards.
    • Variation within and between groups: IQ scores vary more within any group than between groups. Personal and sociocultural biases can affect how an individual's score is interpreted.
    • Inequity: poverty, discrimination and unequal access to education can lower the intelligence scores of individuals and groups around the world.
    • Misuse: intelligence test scores have been used to limit people's access to jobs, military ranks, educational institutions and immigration to the United States.
    Using these on the exam

    If a claim says differences in average IQ between two groups must be genetic, the Flynn effect and the role of poverty, discrimination and educational inequity give you strong evidence to challenge it.

    Part 4

    Achievement, aptitude and mindset

    Achievement test

    Measures what you have already learned.

    Your AP Psychology exam, or a driving theory test.

    Aptitude test

    Predicts how well you will learn or perform in the future.

    A test that predicts how quickly someone could learn a new language.

    Fixed mindset

    The belief that intelligence is set from birth and cannot change much.

    "I'm just not a science person."

    Growth mindset

    The belief that intelligence can grow through effort, strategies and experience.

    "I'm not good at this yet."

    These beliefs matter: students' views about whether intelligence is fixed or can grow are linked to their academic achievement, partly because they shape how students respond to difficulty and mistakes.

    Part 5

    Common mix-ups

    Reliability

    Consistent results.

    Validity

    Measures what it claims to.
    Reliable but not valid is possible; valid but not reliable is not.

    Test-retest reliability

    Same people, same test, two different times.

    Split-half reliability

    Comparing two halves of one test.
    Two sittings: test-retest. One sitting split in two: split-half.

    Construct validity

    Does it measure the intended concept?

    Predictive validity

    Does it predict future performance?
    "Predicts" in the question usually signals predictive validity.

    Achievement test

    What you know now.

    Aptitude test

    What you could do in the future.
    End-of-unit tests are achievement tests.

    Stereotype threat

    Fear of confirming a stereotype lowers performance.

    Stereotype lift

    Comparison with a stereotyped group raises performance.
    Threat pulls down; lift pushes up.

    125. Mental age 10 divided by chronological age 8, times 100.
    2. Students who take the same test twice, two weeks apart, get very similar scores. What does this show?
    Test-retest reliability. The test gives consistent results over time.
