    What you need to be able to do

    Look at a photo of yourself at age five. In some ways you are a completely different person; in others, you are obviously still you. Developmental psychology studies how people grow and change across the whole lifespan, and this topic introduces the big questions it keeps asking and the two main ways it studies change.

    These notes cover Topics 3.1, 3.2 and 3.4 to 3.9. Topic 3.3 is not part of these materials.

    By the end of this page, you should be able to:

    • Explain the difference between studying development in chronological order and by theme.
    • Explain the three enduring themes: stability and change, nature and nurture, and continuous and discontinuous development.
    • Compare cross-sectional and longitudinal research designs, including their strengths and weaknesses.
    How this shows up on the exam

    Expect to identify a theme from a research question ("Do shy toddlers become shy adults?" is stability and change) and to identify or evaluate a research design. The classic trap is forgetting that cross-sectional studies can be distorted by cohort effects, while longitudinal studies lose participants over time.

    Part 1

    Three enduring themes

    Developmental psychologists study development in two ways: in chronological order (infancy, then childhood, then adolescence, and so on) and by theme (big questions that run across every age). Three themes come up again and again:

    Tap a blurred cell to reveal it.
    ThemeThe question it asksExample research question
    Stability and changeWhich traits stay the same across life, and which change?Will a shy toddler become a shy adult?
    Nature and nurtureHow do genes and experience interact to shape development? (Topic 1.1)Is a child's talent for languages inherited, or the result of growing up bilingual?
    Continuous and discontinuous developmentDoes development happen gradually and steadily (continuous), or in distinct stages with sudden shifts (discontinuous)?Do children's thinking skills improve a little every day, or jump from one stage to the next?
    Picture it

    Continuous development is a ramp: a smooth, steady climb. Discontinuous development is a staircase: flat stretches, then a step up to a new stage. Piaget's theory (Topic 3.4) and Erikson's (Topic 3.6) are staircases.

    Part 2

    Cross-sectional and longitudinal designs

    To study change over time, researchers use two main designs.

    Cross-sectional design

    Compares different people of different ages at one point in time. Quick and inexpensive.

    Weakness: the cohort effect. People born in different eras grew up with different schooling, technology and culture, so age differences may really be generation differences.

    Test 20-, 40- and 60-year-olds this week.

    Longitudinal design

    Follows the same people and tests them repeatedly over a long time. Shows real change within individuals.

    Weaknesses: slow and expensive, and attrition: participants drop out over the years, and the ones who stay may not be typical.

    Test the same group at ages 20, 40 and 60.

    Classic trap

    A cross-sectional study finds that older adults score lower on a computer-based problem-solving test than young adults. Before concluding that problem-solving declines with age, ask: did the older group grow up with computers? That is a cohort effect, and a longitudinal design avoids it.

    Part 3

    Common mix-ups

    Cross-sectional

    Different people, different ages, one time.

    Longitudinal

    Same people, tested many times, over years.
    Same people over time? Longitudinal. One snapshot of many ages? Cross-sectional.

    Cohort effect

    A weakness of cross-sectional designs: generation, not age, explains the difference.

    Attrition

    A weakness of longitudinal designs: participants drop out.
    Match each weakness to its design.

    Stability and change

    Does a trait persist across life?

    Continuous and discontinuous

    Is change gradual, or in stages?
    Both are about change over time; ask whether the question is about a trait lasting or about the pattern of change.

    Answer each one in your head (or out loud) before you open it.

    1. A researcher asks whether children who are anxious at age 3 are still anxious at age 30. Which theme is this?
    Stability and change. The question is whether a trait persists across the lifespan.
    2. To study memory and age, a team tests 500 people aged 20 to 80 in a single month. Which design is this?
    Cross-sectional. Different people of different ages are compared at one point in time.
    3. In that study, the oldest group performs worst on a smartphone-based memory test. Why might that not prove that memory declines with age?
    The cohort effect. The oldest group grew up without smartphones, so unfamiliarity with the device, not age itself, could explain the difference.
    4. A study follows 1,000 babies until age 40. By the end, only 400 are still taking part. What problem does this create?
    Attrition. The people who stayed may differ from those who left, which can bias the results of a longitudinal study.
    5. A theory says children's thinking moves through four distinct stages, each very different from the last. Continuous or discontinuous?
    Discontinuous. Development happens in distinct stages, like steps on a staircase.

    What you need to be able to do

    Your body has been changing since before you were born, and it will keep changing for the rest of your life. This topic follows physical development from the womb to old age, and asks how those changes affect behavior and mental processes.

    By the end of this page, you should be able to:

    • Explain the prenatal influences on development: teratogens, maternal illness, genetic mutations, hormones and environmental factors.
    • Explain motor development, infant reflexes, the visual cliff, critical and sensitive periods, and imprinting.
    • Describe the physical changes of adolescence and adulthood.
    How this shows up on the exam

    Most questions apply a term to a scenario: a newborn turning toward a touch on the cheek (rooting reflex), a baby refusing to crawl over the "cliff" (depth perception), a duckling following a person (imprinting). Know the difference between a critical period and a sensitive period.

    Not on the exam

    The stages of prenatal development (zygote, embryo and fetus).

    Before birth

    Before birth, several factors can influence the major physical and psychological milestones of development:

    Part 2

    Infancy and childhood

    Physical development in infancy and childhood follows generally the same order for everyone, but the timing varies from child to child. These skills let children become steadily more independent.

    Gross motor skills

    Large movements using big muscle groups.

    Rolling over, sitting up, crawling, walking, kicking a ball.

    Fine motor skills

    Small, precise movements, especially of the hands and fingers.

    Picking up a raisin, holding a crayon, buttoning a shirt.

    Reflexes and early research

    • Infant reflexes are automatic responses that show development is on track. In the rooting reflex, a baby turns toward a touch on the cheek and opens its mouth, ready to feed.
    • The visual cliff is a glass-topped table with a checkered pattern that appears to drop away halfway across. Most crawling babies refuse to cross the "drop", even when a parent calls them, showing they can perceive depth early in life. It was also an inventive way to study babies who cannot yet answer questions.

    Critical and sensitive periods

    Critical period

    A strict window early in life when a certain experience must happen for normal development.

    Some animals imprint: newly hatched ducklings and geese follow the first moving object they see, normally their mother, which helps them survive.

    Sensitive period

    A window when learning a skill is much easier, but still possible later.

    Children pick up a second language with a native accent far more easily than adults do.

    Part 3

    Adolescence and adulthood

    Adolescence

    Adolescence brings the adolescent growth spurt (a rapid increase in height and weight) and puberty, the period when the body matures and reproductive ability develops.

    • Primary sex characteristics are the body structures directly involved in reproduction.
    • Secondary sex characteristics are other physical changes of puberty that are not directly involved in reproduction, such as a deepening voice or the growth of body hair.
    • Menarche (a girl's first menstrual period) and spermarche (a boy's first ejaculation) are milestones that mark the development of reproductive ability.

    Adulthood

    Adulthood is most of the lifespan. Physical abilities generally level off and then gradually decline, at different rates for different people. Changes include:

    • a decline in reproductive ability, including menopause, the end of menstruation;
    • reduced mobility and flexibility;
    • slower reaction time;
    • less sharp vision and hearing.
    Think behavior, not just biology

    The exam asks how physical changes relate to behavior. Slower reaction times can affect how safely an older adult drives; the growth spurt can affect a teenager's coordination in sport for a while.

    Part 4

    Common mix-ups

    Critical period

    The experience must happen in this window.

    Sensitive period

    Learning is easiest in this window, but possible later.
    Imprinting is the classic critical period; language is usually described as a sensitive period.

    Gross motor skills

    Large movements: crawling, walking.

    Fine motor skills

    Small, precise movements: holding a pencil.
    Big muscles versus small muscles.

    Primary sex characteristics

    Directly involved in reproduction.

    Secondary sex characteristics

    Other changes of puberty, not directly reproductive.
    A deepening voice is secondary.

    What you need to be able to do

    Hide a toy under a blanket in front of a six-month-old and she acts as if it has vanished from the universe. A few years later, the same child will argue with you about what is fair. This topic explains how thinking develops, from infancy to old age.

    By the end of this page, you should be able to:

    • Explain Piaget's ideas of schemas, assimilation and accommodation.
    • Describe Piaget's four stages and the abilities and errors that mark each one.
    • Explain Vygotsky's ideas of social learning, scaffolding and the zone of proximal development.
    • Explain how crystallized and fluid intelligence change in adulthood, and what dementia is.
    How this shows up on the exam

    The most common task: identify a child's Piaget stage from a behavior ("he thinks there is more juice in the tall glass" is preoperational, failing conservation). Remember that the ages are approximate guides, not cut-offs. Vygotsky questions usually describe an adult helping a learner just enough.

    Part 1

    Piaget: four stages of thinking

    Jean Piaget argued that children actively build their understanding of the world through schemas, mental frameworks that organize experience. They update them in two ways (you met these in Topic 2.2):

    Assimilation

    Fitting new information into an existing schema without changing it.

    A toddler sees a camel and calls it a horse.

    Accommodation

    Changing a schema to fit new information.

    She learns that camels are a different animal.

    Piaget saw development as both continuous (schemas are constantly being adjusted) and discontinuous (thinking moves through four distinct stages). Tap a stage to explore it.

    Sensorimotor stage (infancy to toddlerhood)

    Babies learn about the world through their senses and actions: looking, grabbing, mouthing, dropping. The key achievement is object permanence, understanding that things still exist when you cannot see them. Before it develops, out of sight really is out of mind, which is why peek-a-boo is so thrilling.

    Preoperational stage (toddlerhood to early childhood)

    Children become skilled at using mental symbols: words, images and pretend play (a banana becomes a phone). The stage is known more for what children cannot yet do, and the errors they make:

    • No conservation: pour juice from a short, wide glass into a tall, thin one, and the child says there is now more.
    • No reversibility: they cannot mentally undo an action (pour the juice back).
    • Animism: believing objects have feelings ("the table hurt me").
    • Egocentrism: difficulty seeing things from someone else's point of view.

    Children begin to develop a theory of mind in this stage: understanding that other people have thoughts and beliefs different from their own.

    Concrete operational stage (early to late childhood)

    Children can now correct the errors of the preoperational stage: they understand conservation and reversibility, and think logically about real, concrete situations. But they still struggle to think systematically about abstract or hypothetical ideas.

    Formal operational stage (late childhood through adulthood)

    People gain the ability to think abstractly and hypothetically: reasoning about "what if?" questions, justice, or ideas they have never experienced, and testing possibilities systematically. Piaget proposed that not everyone reaches formal operational thinking.

    Part 2

    Vygotsky: learning with others

    Lev Vygotsky emphasized that children are social learners. They learn by interacting with other people, within their culture.

    • The zone of proximal development (ZPD) is the gap between what a learner can do alone and what they can do with guidance. Ideally, learning happens here.
    • Scaffolding is the temporary support a more skilled person gives within the ZPD: hints, questions, demonstrations. Like scaffolding on a building, it is gradually removed as the learner becomes independent.
    Piaget versus Vygotsky

    Piaget pictured the child as a little scientist, discovering the world mostly alone. Vygotsky pictured the child as an apprentice, learning alongside more skilled people.

    Part 3

    Thinking in adulthood

    Thinking keeps changing in adulthood, in two different directions:

    Crystallized intelligence

    Your accumulated knowledge, vocabulary and skills. It stays relatively stable through adulthood and can keep growing.

    An older professor's vast knowledge of her subject.

    Fluid intelligence

    Your ability to reason quickly and solve new problems. It tends to decline as people age.

    Learning a brand-new app quickly.

    Dementia is a cognitive disorder affecting adults, marked by a decline in memory and thinking severe enough to interfere with daily life. Alzheimer's disease (Topic 2.5) is a common cause.

    Part 4

    Common mix-ups

    Conservation

    Understanding that quantity stays the same when shape changes.

    Object permanence

    Understanding that objects exist when out of sight.
    Object permanence is sensorimotor; conservation arrives in the concrete operational stage.

    Egocentrism (preoperational)

    Can't take another person's point of view.

    Theory of mind

    Understanding that others have their own thoughts and beliefs.
    Theory of mind is what replaces egocentrism.

    Concrete operational

    Logical about real, concrete things.

    Formal operational

    Can reason abstractly and hypothetically.
    "What if..." questions point to formal operational thinking.

    Crystallized intelligence

    Knowledge; stable or rising with age.

    Fluid intelligence

    Quick reasoning; tends to decline with age.
    Crystals last; fluids flow away.

    What you need to be able to do

    With a small set of sounds and rules, you can say a sentence that nobody in history has ever said before, and someone who speaks your language will understand it instantly. This topic looks at what language is made of and how every child, in every culture, learns it.

    By the end of this page, you should be able to:

    • Explain the building blocks of language: phonemes, morphemes, semantics, grammar and syntax.
    • Explain why language is described as generative.
    • Describe the stages of language development, and explain overgeneralization.
    How this shows up on the exam

    Expect to identify a building block ("the 's' in 'cats' is a morpheme") or a stage of development from a description, and to explain an overgeneralization error such as "I goed to the park".

    Not on the exam

    Pragmatics (the social rules of how language is used in conversation).

    Part 1

    The building blocks of language

    Language is a shared system of arbitrary symbols, combined according to rules, that can produce an endless number of ideas.

    Tap a blurred cell to reveal it.
    Building blockWhat it isExample
    PhonemeThe smallest unit of sound in a language.The sounds b, a and t in "bat". Arabic has phonemes English does not, such as the sound of the letter ع (ayn).
    MorphemeThe smallest unit of meaning."Unhelpful" has three: un + help + ful. The "s" in "cats" is a morpheme meaning "more than one".
    SemanticsThe rules for deriving meaning from words and sentences.Knowing that "-ed" means something happened in the past.
    GrammarThe whole system of rules that lets us communicate and understand each other.All the rules of English or of Arabic.
    SyntaxThe rules for ordering words into sentences.In English, "the white house", not "the house white". In Arabic, the adjective comes after the noun.

    Language is generative: from a limited set of words and rules, we can produce and understand an infinite number of new sentences.

    Memory hook

    Phoneme = phone, sound. Morpheme = meaning. Syntax = sequence.

    Part 2

    How language develops

    Children in every culture move through the same stages, in the same order.

    Tap a blurred cell to reveal it.
    StageRoughly whenWhat it sounds like
    GesturesFrom infancyNonverbal communication such as pointing, used across all cultures.
    CooingAround 2 monthsSoft vowel sounds: "ooo", "aah".
    BabblingAround 4 to 6 monthsRepeated consonant and vowel sounds: "ba-ba-ba", "da-da". At first babies babble sounds from many languages; gradually they narrow to the sounds of the language around them.
    One-word stageAround 12 monthsSingle words that carry a whole idea: "Milk!" can mean "I want milk now".
    Telegraphic speechAround 2 yearsShort phrases of key words, like an old telegram: "Want cookie." "Daddy go car."

    Overgeneralization: a smart mistake

    As children learn the rules of their language, they often apply them too widely. A child who learns that "-ed" makes the past tense says "I goed to the park" or "I runned"; one who learns that "-s" makes a plural talks about "mouses". This is overgeneralization. It is actually evidence of learning: no adult says "goed", so the child is not copying, but applying a rule.

    Part 3

    Common mix-ups

    Phoneme

    A unit of sound, with no meaning on its own.

    Morpheme

    A unit of meaning.
    The "s" sound is a phoneme; the plural "-s" is a morpheme.

    Syntax

    Word order.

    Semantics

    Meaning.
    "Colorless green ideas sleep furiously" has correct syntax but no sensible semantics.

    Babbling

    Repeated consonant and vowel sounds, no real words.

    Telegraphic speech

    Two or three key words together.
    Babbling comes before any words; telegraphic speech comes after the one-word stage.

    What you need to be able to do

    Who you are is shaped by who is around you: the family that raised you, the friends you choose, the culture you grew up in, even decisions made in offices you have never visited. This topic follows social and emotional development from a baby's first bond to an older adult looking back on life.

    By the end of this page, you should be able to:

    • Explain Bronfenbrenner's ecological systems theory and its five systems.
    • Compare parenting styles and attachment styles, and explain temperament, separation anxiety and Harlow's research.
    • Explain peer relationships in childhood and adolescence, including the imaginary audience and the personal fable.
    • Explain the social clock, emerging adulthood and adult relationships.
    • Describe Erikson's eight psychosocial stages, adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) and the four identity statuses.
    How this shows up on the exam

    This is one of the most-missed topics in Unit 1 to 3. The common traps: mixing up the mesosystem (links between your settings) and the exosystem (settings you are not in that still affect you), and matching an Erikson stage to the wrong age. Expect a scenario and a question asking which system, style, stage or status it shows.

    Not on the exam

    Freud's stage theory of development (Erikson's psychosocial stages are on the exam).

    Part 1

    Ecological systems theory

    Urie Bronfenbrenner's ecological systems theory explains how the social environment influences development, using five nested systems. Tap a ring to explore it.

    Microsystem

    The groups that have direct contact with you: your family, friends, teachers, classmates, sports team.

    Mesosystem

    The relationships between the groups in your microsystem. Your parents meeting your teachers at a parent conference, or your friends getting along (or not) with your family.

    Exosystem

    Indirect factors: settings you are not part of that still affect you. Your father's workplace changing his hours, so he is home less; a local decision about funding for your school.

    Macrosystem

    The wider culture and cultural events that affect you and everyone around you: shared values (such as respect for elders), the economy, a pandemic that closes schools.

    Chronosystem

    Your current stage of life, and how the timing of events across your life shapes their effect. Moving to a new country affects a 6-year-old differently from a 16-year-old.

    Meso or exo?

    Mesosystem: two of your settings interacting (home and school). Exosystem: a setting you are not in that still reaches you (a parent's job).

    Parenting and attachment

    Parenting styles

    Tap a blurred cell to reveal it.
    StyleDemands and rulesWarmth and responsivenessSounds like

    AuthoritativeHigh: clear rules, explainedHigh"Here's the rule, and here's why it matters."

    Research in many Western countries links authoritative parenting with the best outcomes, but cultural differences exist in how each style affects children. In some cultures, strict, high-control parenting is seen as a sign of care and is linked with fewer negative outcomes.

    Attachment

    Attachment is the emotional bond between a child and a caregiver. Researchers often study it by briefly separating toddlers from their caregiver in an unfamiliar room and watching the reunion. Attachment styles vary by culture.

    Tap a blurred cell to reveal it.
    StyleWhen the caregiver leaves and returns, the child...
    SecureIs upset when the caregiver leaves, comforted quickly when they return, and uses them as a safe base to explore.
    Insecure: avoidantShows little distress when the caregiver leaves and ignores or avoids them on return.
    Insecure: anxiousIs very distressed when the caregiver leaves, and clingy but hard to comfort, even angry, on return.

    • Temperament, a child's natural emotional style (easygoing, intense, slow to warm up), is related to how children attach to caregivers.
    • Separation anxiety is heightened fear or distress when a child is away from a caregiver or near a stranger. It is a normal stage in infancy.
    • Harlow's monkey studies: infant monkeys given a choice between a wire "mother" that provided milk and a soft cloth "mother" that provided none spent most of their time clinging to the cloth one, especially when frightened. Attachment depends on comfort, not just food.
    Part 3

    Peers, identity and adulthood

    Childhood and adolescence

    • Young children engage with peers through play: parallel play (playing side by side without really interacting, typical of toddlers) and pretend play (acting out roles and imaginary situations).
    • Adolescents rely more and more on their peers. They also show a special type of egocentrism:
      • the imaginary audience: feeling that everyone is watching and judging you ("Everyone noticed my bad hair today");
      • the personal fable: believing your experiences are unique and that bad things will not happen to you ("No one has ever felt what I feel"; "I won't get hurt").

    Identity

    Adolescents develop a sense of who they will be as adults. One model describes four identity statuses, based on whether a person has explored options and whether they have committed to one:

    Has made a commitmentHas not made a commitment
    Has explored optionsAchievement: explored, then chose. "I tried several subjects and chose engineering because it suits me."Moratorium: actively exploring, not yet decided. "I'm trying out different clubs to see what fits."
    Has not exploredForeclosure: committed without exploring, often to others' expectations. "I'll be a doctor because my family expects it."Diffusion: neither exploring nor committed. "I don't really think about it."

    Identity has many parts, such as occupational, family, religious and ethnic identity. Young people often develop them by imagining their possible selves: who they might become, or fear becoming.

    Adulthood

    • Culture shapes when adulthood begins and when major life events (finishing study, starting work, marrying, having children) are expected. This culturally expected timetable is the social clock.
    • Some cultures allow a period of emerging adulthood, roughly from the late teens to the mid-twenties, as a transition between adolescence and full adult responsibility.
    • Adults form families or family-like relationships that should provide mutual support and care. Childhood attachment styles can affect how adults form attachments to other adults.
    Part 4

    Adverse childhood experiences are potentially traumatic or highly stressful events in childhood, such as abuse, neglect or serious difficulties at home. ACEs can affect the relationships people form throughout their lives. What counts as an ACE, and how strongly it affects a person, differs across sociocultural contexts.

    What you need to be able to do

    Why does your mouth water when you hear a particular kettle whistle? Why does the smell of a dentist's office make some people tense? You learned those reactions without trying. This topic explains classical conditioning: learning to link one thing with another.

    By the end of this page, you should be able to:

    • Explain the behavioral perspective and the components of classical conditioning (NS, UCS, UCR, CS, CR).
    • Explain acquisition (and why order matters), extinction, spontaneous recovery, generalization, discrimination and higher-order conditioning.
    • Explain conditioned emotional responses and counterconditioning.
    • Explain taste aversion, one-trial learning, biological preparedness and habituation.
    How this shows up on the exam

    The core skill is labeling UCS, UCR, CS and CR in a brand-new scenario. A reliable trick: ask what produced the response before any learning happened. That is the UCS. Expect graph questions on acquisition, extinction and spontaneous recovery too.

    Not on the exam

    Delayed, trace, simultaneous and backward conditioning, and expectancy theory.

    Part 1

    How classical conditioning works

    The behavioral perspective grew out of research on learning through conditioning. Behaviorists traditionally focused on observable behavior and left out mental processes they could not observe directly.

    Classical conditioning is learning to associate two stimuli, so that one starts to trigger a response the other already produced. Ivan Pavlov discovered it when his dogs began to drool at the sound that came before their food. Here is a version closer to home:

    1. Before conditioning

    Karak (UCS) makes Reem's mouth water (UCR).

    The kettle whistle is a neutral stimulus (NS): it causes no watering.

    2. During conditioning

    Every afternoon, the whistle (NS) sounds just before the karak (UCS) arrives, and her mouth waters (UCR).

    Repeated pairings: acquisition.

    3. After conditioning

    Now the whistle alone (CS) makes her mouth water (CR).

    The NS has become a conditioned stimulus.

    TermMeaningIn Reem's case
    Unconditioned stimulus (UCS)Naturally triggers a response, with no learning needed.The karak
    Unconditioned response (UCR)The natural, unlearned response to the UCS.Mouth watering at the karak
    Neutral stimulus (NS)Causes no relevant response before conditioning.The whistle, at first
    Conditioned stimulus (CS)The former NS, which now triggers the response after being paired with the UCS.The whistle, after conditioning

    The labeling routine

    1. What caused the response before any learning? That is the UCS, and the response is the UCR. 2. What was paired with it? That is the NS, which becomes the CS. 3. The same response, now triggered by the CS, is the CR. The UCR and CR are usually the same behavior; what differs is what triggers it.

    Part 2

    Acquisition, extinction and more

    Tap a blurred cell to reveal it.
    ProcessWhat happensIn Reem's case
    AcquisitionThe association is learned through repeated pairings. Order matters: the NS should come just before the UCS.Whistle, then karak, many times.
    ExtinctionThe CR fades when the CS keeps appearing without the UCS.Her family switches to an electric kettle for coffee only; the whistle no longer means karak, and the watering stops.
    Spontaneous recoveryAfter a rest, an extinguished CR suddenly reappears (usually weaker) when the CS returns.Months later at her grandmother's, the old whistle makes her mouth water again.
    GeneralizationStimuli similar to the CS also trigger the CR.A similar whistle on a TV show makes her mouth water.
    DiscriminationLearning to respond only to the CS, not to similar stimuli.Only her grandmother's particular kettle does it.
    Higher-order conditioningA CS is used like a UCS to condition a new stimulus.Her grandmother's car always arrives just before the whistle; now the car's sound alone makes her mouth water.
    Extinction is not erasing

    Spontaneous recovery shows that extinction suppresses the response rather than deleting the association.

    Part 3

    Emotions, therapy and biological limits

    Conditioned emotions

    Emotional responses can be classically conditioned. In a famous and now-criticized study, John Watson and Rosalie Rayner conditioned an infant known as "Little Albert" to fear a white rat by pairing it with a sudden loud noise. The fear generalized to other furry objects. Today the study is a classic example of research that would break ethical guidelines, because it deliberately caused distress to a child and did not remove the fear afterwards.

    The same principle is used to help people. Counterconditioning replaces an unwanted conditioned response with a new one: a person afraid of dogs is gradually exposed to dogs while feeling calm and relaxed, until the dogs become linked with calm instead of fear. Therapies for many disorders build on this (Unit 5).

    Biology sets limits

    Taste aversion

    A strong dislike of a food after it was followed by illness. It shows one-trial learning (one pairing is enough, and further pairings do not strengthen it) and works even when the illness comes hours later.

    Sick after a seafood dinner once? You may avoid that dish for years.

    Biological preparedness

    Animals are biologically predisposed to learn some associations much more easily than others: taste with illness, or fear of snakes and heights, because these helped survival.

    Rats link sickness to a taste, not to a light or a sound.

    Habituation is a related form of learning: a reduced response to a stimulus that is repeated or continues without consequence. You stop jumping at the construction noise next door after a few days.

    Part 4

    Common mix-ups

    UCS

    Triggers the response with no learning.

    CS

    Triggers the response only after learning.
    If it needed pairing to work, it is the CS.

    Extinction

    The CR fades without the UCS.

    Spontaneous recovery

    The CR returns after a rest.
    Recovery needs a pause after extinction.

    Generalization

    Responding to similar stimuli too.

    Discrimination

    Responding only to the exact CS.
    Generalization spreads; discrimination narrows.

    Habituation

    A learned drop in response to a repeated stimulus.

    Sensory adaptation (1.6)

    Receptors become less sensitive to an unchanging stimulus.
    Habituation is learning; sensory adaptation happens at the senses.

    What you need to be able to do

    Why do you keep checking your phone, even when there is rarely anything new? Why does a toddler throw a bigger tantrum when it worked last time? Operant conditioning explains how the consequences of our behavior shape what we do next.

    By the end of this page, you should be able to:

    The single most reliable routine: ask whether the behavior increases (reinforcement) or decreases (punishment), then whether something was added (positive) or removed (negative). Negative reinforcement is not punishment. Expect graph questions on schedules (fixed interval makes a scalloped pattern).

    Part 1

    Consequences shape behavior

    In operant conditioning, we learn to associate a behavior with its consequences. Edward Thorndike's Law of Effect states that behaviors followed by satisfying consequences are more likely to be repeated, and behaviors followed by unpleasant consequences are less likely. B. F. Skinner built on this to study how consequences shape behavior.

    Behavior increases: reinforcementBehavior decreases: punishment
    Something added: positivePositive reinforcement
    Adding something pleasant.

    Positive and negative are math, not good and bad

    Positive means add (+). Negative means subtract (minus). Taking a pain reliever because it removes a headache is negative reinforcement: something unpleasant is subtracted, and you are more likely to take it next time.

    Primary reinforcers

    Satisfy biological needs, with no learning required.

    Food, water, warmth.

    Secondary reinforcers

    Gain their power through association with primary reinforcers.

    Money, grades, stars on a chart, a coach's praise.

    Reinforcement discrimination is learning that a behavior is reinforced in one situation but not another (asking for sweets works with grandma, not with mom). Reinforcement generalization is repeating a reinforced behavior in similar situations (saying "please" everywhere because it worked at home).

    Part 3

    Schedules of reinforcement

    How often reinforcement comes determines how strongly and steadily a behavior is learned.

    Continuous reinforcement

    Every correct behavior is reinforced. The fastest way to learn a new behavior, but it also fades fastest once reinforcement stops.

    A treat every time the puppy sits.

    Partial reinforcement

    Only some behaviors are reinforced. Learning is slower, but the behavior is much more resistant to extinction.

    A treat only some of the times.

    Partial schedules differ in two ways: is reinforcement based on number of responses (ratio) or time (interval)? And is it predictable (fixed) or unpredictable (variable)?

    Is it about how many (ratio) or how long (interval)? Can you predict it (fixed) or not (variable)?

    What you need to be able to do

    You probably learned to tie your shoes by watching someone, not by being rewarded step by step. And you know the way around your neighborhood even though nobody ever trained you. This topic covers the kinds of learning that classical and operant conditioning cannot fully explain.

    By the end of this page, you should be able to:

    • Explain social learning theory: observational learning, modeling and vicarious conditioning.
    • Explain insight learning.
    • Explain latent learning and cognitive maps.
    How this shows up on the exam

    Expect scenarios that ask you to tell observational, insight and latent learning apart, often alongside classical and operant conditioning. The comparison table below is the quickest way to sort them.

    Part 1

    Learning by watching

    Albert Bandura's social learning theory proposes that we can learn by observation, without personally experiencing a consequence.

    • Modeling: we learn by watching and copying the behavior of others (models).
    • Vicarious conditioning: we learn from the consequences that others receive. Seeing a classmate praised for a creative answer (vicarious reinforcement) makes you more likely to try one; seeing a teammate benched for arguing with the referee (vicarious punishment) makes you less likely to argue.
    • The more similar a model is to us, the more likely we are to learn from them.

    In Bandura's Bobo doll experiments, children who watched an adult hit and kick an inflatable doll were much more likely to attack the doll themselves, often copying the adult's exact actions and words.

    Part 2

    Cognitive factors in learning

    Insight learning

    The solution to a problem appears suddenly, without trial and error, reinforcement or a model: the "aha!" moment. Wolfgang Köhler described a chimpanzee named Sultan who, after failing to reach bananas, suddenly stacked boxes and used a stick to get them.

    A puzzle you stared at for ages, then suddenly saw the answer.

    Latent learning

    Learning that happens without reinforcement and stays hidden until there is a reason to show it. Edward Tolman found that rats who explored a maze without food later ran it as fast as rats trained with food, as soon as food was added. They had formed a cognitive map, a mental picture of the layout.

    Being driven to school for years, then easily giving directions when a friend needs them.

    These kinds of learning also have a neurological side. Learning strengthens connections between neurons through long-term potentiation (Topic 2.3) and reshapes the brain through plasticity (Topic 1.4).
